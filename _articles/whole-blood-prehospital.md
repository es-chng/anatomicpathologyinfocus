article_type: "Evidence brief"

question: >-
  Does prehospital transfusion of up to 2 units of cold-stored, leukoreduced
  whole blood improve survival compared with standard component therapy
  (red cells and plasma) in trauma patients with hemorrhagic shock?

background: >-
  Hemorrhagic shock remains the leading cause of preventable death after
  injury. Over the past two decades, early administration of blood products
  has transformed care for bleeding patients. The PROPPR trial established
  that balanced blood-component therapy (plasma, platelets, and red cells in
  equal ratios) improved hemostasis and reduced death from early hemorrhage.
  Prehospital plasma subsequently showed a survival benefit in air medical
  transport but not in rapid ground transportation. Whole blood has gained
  interest as a simpler way to deliver balanced hemostatic resuscitation:
  it combines red cells, plasma, and platelets in a single unit, streamlines
  logistics, shortens administration time, and reduces transfusion errors.
  However, high-level evidence on the efficacy and safety of prehospital
  whole blood was limited. Two phase 3 randomized trials—the Study of Whole
  Blood in Frontline Trauma (SWiFT) and the Type O Whole Blood and Assessment
  of Age during Prehospital Resuscitation (TOWAR) trial—were conducted to
  address this gap.

evidence:
  - source: "TOWAR trial, Sperry et al. (2026); 1020 trauma patients, 44 air medical bases, 2:1 cluster randomization"
    finding: "Mortality at 30 days was 25.9% in the whole-blood group (180 of 695 patients) and 20.5% in the component group (61 of 298 patients); adjusted odds ratio 1.24 (95% CI 0.87 to 1.76; P = 0.24). No substantial between-group differences were observed in secondary outcomes, including death at 3, 6, and 24 hours, in-hospital death, units of blood products transfused within 24 hours, multiple organ failure, nosocomial infection, or acute respiratory distress syndrome. In the observational substudy, 30-day mortality was 27.1% among 210 patients receiving whole blood stored 15 to 21 days versus 26.4% among 443 receiving blood stored 1 to 14 days (adjusted OR 0.99; 95% CI 0.74 to 1.32)."
    limitation: "The trial was powered to detect a 10-percentage-point absolute difference in 30-day mortality (26% vs 16%); it was not designed as an equivalence trial, and the confidence intervals do not exclude clinically meaningful differences in either direction. Baseline mortality was lower than in earlier prehospital trials, so any true difference was likely smaller than the trial was powered to detect. Traumatic brain injury was present in 39.7% of patients, and hemorrhage was the reported cause of death in only 9.0% of the whole-blood group and 7.8% of the component group—so the cohort may not have been enriched for patients most likely to die from bleeding."
  - source: "SWiFT trial, Smith et al. (2026); 616 analyzable patients, 10 air ambulance services in England"
    finding: "The primary composite outcome of death from any cause or massive transfusion (≥10 units) within 24 hours occurred in 48.7% of the whole-blood group (153 of 314) and 47.7% of the standard-care group (144 of 302); adjusted relative risk 1.02 (95% CI 0.80 to 1.31; P = 0.84). Death from any cause at 6 hours, 24 hours, 30 days, and 90 days, the incidence of massive transfusion, and other secondary clinical outcomes appeared similar between groups. Prothrombin time above the normal range occurred more often in the whole-blood group (40.7% vs 30.5%; RR 1.31; 95% CI 1.10 to 1.56), which did not translate into differences in clinical outcomes. Slightly more serious adverse events occurred in the standard-care group (37 events) than in the whole-blood group (31 events); thrombotic event rates were similar."
    limitation: "The trial was powered to detect an absolute difference of 12 percentage points (68% vs 56%) in the composite primary outcome; the observed event rate was substantially lower than anticipated, so the trial may have been underpowered for smaller but clinically relevant differences. The pragmatic design relied on clinical judgment to initiate transfusion, which may have introduced population heterogeneity and included some patients without life-threatening hemorrhage. A cyberattack on the Trauma Audit and Research Network database in 2023 resulted in some missing injury-characteristic data."
  - source: "Both trials; product characteristics and dose"
    finding: "Both trials used up to 2 units of cold-stored, leukoreduced whole blood. Cold-stored, leukoreduced whole blood is not the warm fresh whole blood associated with survival benefits in military settings: leukoreduction reduces platelet count and impairs platelet function, and storage degrades coagulation factor activity and platelet hemostatic capacity over time. The SWiFT trial used a platelet-sparing filter and produced uniform, platelet-replete units, whereas TOWAR deferred to local blood-banking practices and tested a heterogeneous mix of conventionally leukoreduced and nonleukoreduced units. Nevertheless, both trials reached the same conclusion: 1 or 2 units of cold-stored, leukoreduced whole blood produced outcomes indistinguishable from component therapy."
    limitation: "Protocol-indicated transfusion was capped at 2 units, a volume possibly too small to show benefit. A secondary analysis of PROPPR showed that early delivery of platelets was associated with improved survival, so whether 1 or 2 units of cold-stored whole blood delivered enough platelet activity is unclear. Whole blood is fundamentally a hemostatic therapy, and its benefit should be greatest in patients dying from hemorrhage—yet in TOWAR, hemorrhage was the reported cause of death in only 9.0% of the whole-blood group and 7.8% of the component group."

recommendations:
  - "Whole blood and component therapy appear to perform similarly at the doses studied (up to 2 units), so blood-product selection should be guided by availability, capacity, cost, and operational realities rather than by an expectation of superior survival with whole blood."
  - "Neither trial identified a safety concern with either approach; prehospital systems with established blood programs can safely continue to use either whole blood or component therapy."
  - "Up to 2 units of cold-stored, leukoreduced whole blood does not outperform component therapy; whether higher doses or a more bleeding-enriched cohort would change outcomes remains unknown."
  - "The storage age of whole blood through 21 days from donation was not associated with apparent differences in outcomes in the TOWAR observational substudy, so age-based product selection within the licensed shelf life is not supported by these data."
  - "Clinicians should recognize that the similar outcomes in both trials reflect how dramatically prehospital hemorrhage care has improved—including widespread tourniquet use, hemorrhage-control training, damage-control resuscitation, and prehospital transfusion programs—rather than a failure of the whole-blood strategy itself."

open_questions:
  - "Whether higher doses of whole blood (beyond 2 units) or a cohort more heavily enriched for hemorrhagic death would demonstrate a survival benefit."
  - "Whether specific patient subgroups—such as those with severe hypotension (systolic blood pressure ≤70 mm Hg) or those without traumatic brain injury—might benefit differentially from whole blood, as suggested by exploratory subgroup analyses."
  - "Whether cold-stored, leukoreduced whole blood can be modified to preserve platelet function and coagulation factor potency closer to that of warm fresh whole blood."
  - "Whether the observed prolongation of prothrombin time in the whole-blood group (attributable to older plasma in stored whole blood) has any clinical relevance in other settings or with larger volumes."
  - "How the heterogeneity between the two trials' products—platelet-sparing filtered versus conventionally leukoreduced whole blood—affects generalizability across different blood-banking systems."
  - "Whether the lower-than-expected baseline mortality in contemporary prehospital trauma trials means that future trials must be substantially larger or must target higher-risk cohorts to detect meaningful differences."

perspective: >-
  These two trials should not be misread as evidence that whole blood is a
  failed resuscitation strategy. Rather, they reflect how dramatically
  prehospital care of patients with hemorrhage has improved. The 24% to 28%
  30-day mortality observed in both trials is meaningfully lower than that
  reported in earlier trials conducted before widespread tourniquet use,
  hemorrhage-control training, damage-control resuscitation, and adoption of
  prehospital blood-transfusion programs. Because baseline mortality in both
  trials was lower than in earlier prehospital trials, including PAMPer, any
  true mortality difference was most likely smaller than the trials were
  powered to detect. Neither trial was designed as an equivalence trial, and
  their confidence intervals do not exclude clinically meaningful differences
  in either direction. The similarity of outcomes across two trials with
  strikingly different designs—different whole-blood products against
  different comparison groups, using different randomization strategies, and
  measuring different primary outcomes on two continents—is notable and
  suggests that cold-stored whole blood does not produce a measurable
  survival benefit at the doses tested. Two other characteristics deserve
  emphasis: the 2-unit dose cap may have been too small to show benefit,
  particularly given evidence that early platelet delivery improves survival;
  and neither trial enrolled a cohort enriched for death from uncontrolled
  bleeding, with traumatic brain injury accounting for a substantial
  proportion of deaths in both. The practical takeaway is that blood-product
  selection can now be driven by logistics, cost, and supply rather than by
  an assumption of clinical superiority. Whether higher doses or a more
  bleeding-enriched cohort would change the outcome remains unknown.

bottom_line: >-
  Two phase 3 trials show prehospital whole blood (up to 2 units) is not
  superior to component therapy for traumatic hemorrhage, with similar safety;
  product choice can be guided by logistics and availability.
