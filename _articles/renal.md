article_type: "Pathology framework"

question: >-
  How should a renal glomerular biopsy be approached systematically, from
  light microscopy pattern through immunofluorescence and electron microscopy
  to a final diagnosis?

background: >-
  The light microscopic pattern is a starting point, not a diagnosis.
  Immunofluorescence is the master branch point; electron microscopy resolves
  the remainder; clinical correlation is always required. This framework
  organizes glomerular disease by the pattern seen on light microscopy and
  provides the decision logic for each pattern.

bottom_line: >-
  Light microscopy tells you where to look; immunofluorescence tells you what
  disease family you are in; electron microscopy tells you which member of
  that family it is; and clinical correlation tells you whether the answer
  makes sense.

sections:
  - heading: "Normal Glomeruli by Light Microscopy"
    question: >-
      What diagnoses must be considered when the glomeruli look entirely
      unremarkable on light microscopy?
    content: >-
      This is the most important pitfall category in renal biopsy
      interpretation. A glomerulus that looks entirely unremarkable on light
      microscopy does not mean the biopsy is normal. Both entities in this
      group are electron microscopy-dependent diagnoses. If you sign out
      "normal" based on light microscopy alone without adequate electron
      microscopy evaluation, you will miss both.

      | Feature | Minimal Change Disease | Thin Basement Membrane Nephropathy |
      | --- | --- | --- |
      | Light microscopy | Normal | Normal |
      | Diagnostic finding | Foot process effacement (extensive/diffuse) | Glomerular basement membrane thinning (measured, age-adjusted) |
      | What electron microscopy must show | Effacement — a podocyte/cytoplasmic abnormality | Thin glomerular basement membrane — a structural/collagen abnormality |
      | Sampling requirement | At least 25 glomeruli to exclude unsampled focal segmental glomerulosclerosis | Not applicable — but requires multiple glomerular basement membrane measurements for a valid average |
      | Underlying defect | Presumed immune-mediated/functional (permeability factor) | Structural — COL4-alpha-3/alpha-4 heterozygous mutation |
      | Classic presentation | Nephrotic syndrome, sudden onset | Persistent asymptomatic microscopic hematuria |
      | Age group | Most common in children | Any age; often detected incidentally |
      | Course | Does not cause progressive chronic kidney disease; steroid-responsive | Usually benign, but approximately 30% develop late renal insufficiency/hypertension; approximately 40% are Alport carriers |
      | Superimposed lesion risk | Not applicable | Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis may develop if nephrotic-range proteinuria appears |

      Minimal change disease teaches the importance of adequate sampling —
      normal-appearing glomeruli on light microscopy could simply mean you have
      not sampled the segmentally sclerotic one. Effacement on electron
      microscopy confirms podocyte injury is present, but you still need at
      least 25 glomeruli to be confident you are not missing focal sclerosis.
      Thin basement membrane nephropathy teaches the importance of
      quantitative rigor — you cannot eyeball glomerular basement membrane
      thickness; it must be measured against age-matched norms, using foot
      process width as an internal reference when precise morphometry is
      limited.

      Both entities remind you that normal light microscopy plus an abnormal
      clinical picture — nephrotic syndrome or persistent hematuria — is
      itself an indication for meticulous electron microscopy examination,
      not a reason to defer or abbreviate it.
    bottom_line: >-
      A normal-looking glomerulus on light microscopy is a starting point,
      not an endpoint; minimal change disease and thin basement membrane
      nephropathy are both invisible without electron microscopy, and signing
      out "normal" without it misses both.

  - heading: "Mesangial Proliferation Without Nodules"
    question: >-
      When the light microscopic pattern is mesangial hypercellularity without
      nodule formation, how do you separate the possible entities?
    content: >-
      This category groups entities that share a light microscopic pattern of
      mesangial hypercellularity or proliferation without nodule formation.
      The critical branch point in the workup is immunofluorescence: does the
      mesangial proliferation correspond to immune complex deposits, or not?
      This single question splits the differential into two clean diagnostic
      pathways.

      Branch 1 — mesangial proliferation with immune complex deposits:

      | Entity | Immunofluorescence signature | Electron microscopy signature | Key clinical clue |
      | --- | --- | --- | --- |
      | IgA nephropathy | Dominant or co-dominant mesangial IgA | Mesangial electron-dense deposits | Young patients; synpharyngitic hematuria |
      | Lupus nephritis class II | Full house — IgG, IgM, IgA, C3, C1q | Tubuloreticular aggregates (interferon footprint) | Systemic lupus erythematosus; young women, African Americans |
      | Post-infectious or infection-related glomerulonephritis | IgA- or C3-dominant, starry sky | Subepithelial humps (taller than wide) | Post or active infection; diabetics, comorbidities |

      These three entities can all show IgA positivity by immunofluorescence,
      making them a classic trap. The discriminators are completeness of the
      immunofluorescence panel (full house indicates lupus), electron
      microscopy deposit location and shape (humps indicate infection-related;
      mesangial-only indicates IgA nephropathy), and clinical context.

      Branch 2 — mesangial proliferation without immune complex deposits:

      | Entity | Distinguishing feature |
      | --- | --- |
      | Early diabetic nephropathy | Precedes nodule formation; glomerular basement membrane thickening on electron microscopy, diabetes history, no immune deposits |
      | Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis | Mesangial hypercellularity may be seen in some variants, but look for segmental sclerosis elsewhere and foot process effacement on electron microscopy; no immune complexes |
      | Nonspecific | A wastebasket category — mesangial hypercellularity without a definable specific cause; requires clinical correlation and exclusion of the above |

      When immunofluorescence is negative or nonspecific in a biopsy showing
      mesangial proliferation, the differential shifts entirely away from
      immune-complex disease and toward early sclerosing processes or a truly
      nonspecific reactive pattern — and electron microscopy becomes the
      deciding modality.

      Practical workup: immunofluorescence first. If IgA-dominant, use
      clinical context plus electron microscopy deposit location. If full
      house, lupus nephritis. If negative or nonspecific, proceed to electron
      microscopy for glomerular basement membrane thickness versus foot
      process effacement with possible segmental sclerosis elsewhere. Always
      correlate clinically.
    bottom_line: >-
      In mesangial proliferation without nodules, immunofluorescence is the
      branch point: immune complex deposits send you to IgA nephropathy,
      lupus, or infection-related disease; their absence sends you to early
      diabetic nephropathy, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, or a
      nonspecific pattern — and electron microscopy decides.

  - heading: "Mesangial and Endocapillary Proliferation"
    question: >-
      When the light microscopic pattern combines mesangial and endocapillary
      proliferation, how do you separate the five entities that share this
      appearance?
    content: >-
      This category groups entities that share a combined mesangial and
      endocapillary proliferative pattern on light microscopy — a more
      active-appearing, often more acute-onset pattern than mesangial-only
      proliferation. The unifying light microscopy theme means these five
      entities cannot be separated from each other by light microscopy alone.
      The differential hinges on immunofluorescence pattern (monoclonal
      versus polyclonal, dominant immunoglobulin) and electron microscopy
      ultrastructure (deposit location, shape, and size), always interpreted
      alongside clinical context.

      | Entity | Immunofluorescence pattern | Electron microscopy signature | Key clinical clue |
      | --- | --- | --- | --- |
      | Lupus nephritis III/IV | Full house — IgG, IgM, IgA, C3, C1q | Subendothelial deposits (plus or minus wire loops); tubuloreticular aggregates | Lupus serologies; young women, African Americans |
      | Cryoglobulinemic glomerulonephritis | IgM-dominant or mixed IgG/IgM, kappa-dominant | Microtubular substructures, 25–35 nm, hollow centers; intraluminal cryoplugs on light microscopy | Hepatitis C (type 2, most common renal-involving type); skin and glomerular vasculitis |
      | Post-infectious glomerulonephritis | C3-dominant (plus or minus IgG), starry sky | Subepithelial humps — taller than wide, no basement membrane reaction | Preceding infection (classically streptococcal); latent period; low complement; children more than adults for good prognosis |
      | Fibrillary glomerulonephritis | Polyclonal IgG plus C3, smudgy | Fibrils, 12–20 nm, randomly arranged, nonbranching | DNAJB9 positive; approximately 25% show crescentic or rapidly progressive variant; poor prognosis (approximately 50% end-stage renal disease by 4 years) |
      | Proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits | Monoclonal — single light chain plus single IgG subclass (usually IgG3 kappa) | Mimics ordinary immune-complex glomerulonephritis — no distinctive ultrastructure | Middle-aged white women; monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance (not myeloma precursor); recurs early post-transplant |

      The monoclonal versus polyclonal split is the fastest first branch
      point. Monoclonal immunofluorescence leads to proliferative
      glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits (no
      distinctive electron microscopy) or cryoglobulinemic
      glomerulonephritis (distinctive microtubules). Polyclonal
      immunofluorescence leads to fibrillary glomerulonephritis (fibrils),
      post-infectious glomerulonephritis (humps), or lupus (full house plus
      multiple immunoglobulin and complement components).

      Unlike the mesangial-only category, where immunofluorescence often
      provides the decisive clue, this category leans more heavily on electron
      microscopy ultrastructure to separate look-alikes. Proliferative
      glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits is the
      outlier — it is the only entity here that is a true morphologic
      chameleon, indistinguishable from ordinary immune-complex
      glomerulonephritis by both light microscopy and electron microscopy.
      This is precisely why routine practice must include immunofluorescence
      light-chain and heavy-chain subclass staining whenever an immune-complex
      pattern glomerulonephritis is encountered, rather than assuming
      polyclonality by default.
    bottom_line: >-
      In combined mesangial and endocapillary proliferation, the monoclonal
      versus polyclonal immunofluorescence split is the fastest branch point,
      and electron microscopy does the heavy lifting to separate look-alikes —
      except in proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal
      immunoglobulin deposits, which is a morphologic chameleon diagnosable
      only by immunofluorescence monoclonality.

  - heading: "Membranoproliferative Glomerulonephritis Pattern"
    question: >-
      What does the membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis pattern on light
      microscopy actually represent, and what are its mechanistic categories?
    content: >-
      This confirms and consolidates the membranoproliferative
      glomerulonephritis pattern as the final, unifying light microscopy
      category in the proliferative glomerular lesions series. The morphologic
      definition is deliberately broad and mechanism-agnostic: mesangial
      hypercellularity, with or without endocapillary hypercellularity, and
      splitting (double contour) of the glomerular basement membrane. This
      single pattern is the common downstream output of at least five
      mechanistically distinct disease categories — which is precisely why
      immunofluorescence and sometimes electron microscopy are indispensable
      to sort them out.

      Note that endocapillary hypercellularity is explicitly optional. A
      biopsy can qualify as showing a membranoproliferative
      glomerulonephritis pattern based on mesangial hypercellularity and
      double contours alone, without active endocapillary proliferation. This
      matters because the pattern spans a spectrum from more active and
      proliferative to more chronic and remodeling appearances.

      | Category | Representative diseases | Immunofluorescence signature |
      | --- | --- | --- |
      | Autoimmune disease | Lupus, Sjogren syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, mixed connective tissue disease | Polyclonal immunoglobulin plus C3/C1q (full house in lupus) |
      | Infection-related glomerulonephritis | Hepatitis B/C, endocarditis, shunt infections, visceral abscesses, leprosy, malaria, schistosomiasis, mycoplasma | Polyclonal immunoglobulin plus C3/C1q |
      | Complement regulatory protein abnormalities | C3 glomerulonephritis, dense deposit disease | Isolated C3 (no significant immunoglobulin); split by electron microscopy |
      | Thrombotic microangiopathy | Covered separately under vascular lesions | Fibrin, no immunoglobulin |
      | Paraprotein deposition diseases | Cryoglobulinemia (types 1/2), monoclonal immunoglobulin deposition disease, immunotactoid/fibrillary glomerulopathy | Monoclonal immunoglobulin plus C3/C1q |

      Notice that thrombotic microangiopathy is explicitly cross-referenced
      to vascular lesions rather than fully elaborated here. This is a
      deliberate classification choice: thrombotic microangiopathy can produce
      a membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis-like pattern as chronic
      changes from endothelial injury, but its primary pathogenesis is
      vascular and endothelial, not glomerular and immune-complex-mediated,
      so it is taxonomically housed elsewhere despite occasionally mimicking
      this pattern morphologically.

      The membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis pattern effectively
      absorbs and re-contextualizes nearly every entity already covered:
      lupus nephritis III/IV in the autoimmune branch; cryoglobulinemic
      glomerulonephritis and proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal
      immunoglobulin deposits in the paraprotein deposition branch;
      post-infectious glomerulonephritis and IgA-dominant infection-related
      glomerulonephritis in the infection-related branch, though these more
      classically show a purely endocapillary proliferative or exudative
      pattern rather than double contours; C3 glomerulonephritis and dense
      deposit disease in the complement regulatory protein branch; and
      fibrillary glomerulonephritis, which is technically distinct with
      organized fibrillary deposits rather than a true double-contour
      pattern, but shares the polyclonal IgG/C3 immunofluorescence motif.

      The single decisive teaching point: whenever double contours or
      glomerular basement membrane splitting are seen on light microscopy,
      the diagnostic task is never complete at the light microscopy level.
      Immunofluorescence (immunoglobulin clonality, dominant component),
      electron microscopy (deposit location and character), and
      clinical/serologic correlation (infection screen, autoimmune
      serologies, paraprotein workup, complement studies) are all required to
      assign the correct final diagnosis and, critically, the correct
      treatment pathway.
    bottom_line: >-
      The membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis pattern is a descriptive
      light microscopy term, not a diagnosis; it maps to at least five
      mechanistically distinct disease categories, and double contours on
      light microscopy should always trigger a full immunofluorescence,
      electron microscopy, and clinical/serologic workup rather than a
      premature sign-out.

  - heading: "Immunofluorescence-Based Classification of the Membranoproliferative Glomerulonephritis Pattern"
    question: >-
      How does immunofluorescence sort the membranoproliferative
      glomerulonephritis pattern into its three fundamental disease
      categories?
    content: >-
      This is the capstone organizing principle for the lupus,
      cryoglobulinemic, post-infectious, fibrillary, monoclonal
      immunoglobulin deposition, C3 glomerulonephritis, and dense deposit
      disease discussions. The membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis
      pattern by light microscopy is a shared morphologic endpoint reached by
      mechanistically distinct diseases. Immunofluorescence is the master
      branch point that sorts this single light microscopy pattern into three
      fundamentally different disease categories, and this is the single most
      efficient framework for approaching any membranoproliferative
      glomerulonephritis-pattern biopsy in practice.

      Branch 1 — polyclonal immunoglobulin plus C3/C1q, indicating immune
      complex disease. Genuine antibody-antigen immune complexes, polyclonal
      in nature, driven by an external trigger (infection) or systemic
      autoimmune process. Infection-related examples include hepatitis B,
      hepatitis C, endocarditis, shunt infections, visceral abscesses,
      leprosy, malaria, schistosomiasis, and mycoplasma. Autoimmune-related
      examples include systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjogren syndrome,
      rheumatoid arthritis, and mixed connective tissue disease. This branch
      is a reminder that membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis is very
      often secondary, not idiopathic — a biopsy with polyclonal
      full-house-type immunofluorescence should trigger a broad
      infectious/autoimmune serologic workup, not an assumption of
      primary/idiopathic membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, a diagnosis
      that has become far less common as more secondary causes are
      identified.

      Branch 2 — monoclonal immunoglobulin plus C3/C1q, indicating paraprotein
      deposition disease. Immune complexes or deposits containing a clonal
      immunoglobulin component, reflecting an underlying clonal B-cell or
      plasma cell process spanning the full monoclonal gammopathy of renal
      significance-to-malignancy spectrum. The clonal process may be
      monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, Waldenstrom
      macroglobulinemia, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, or low-grade B-cell
      lymphoma. Specific disease patterns include cryoglobulinemia types 1
      and 2, immunotactoid/fibrillary glomerulopathy, and monoclonal
      immunoglobulin deposition disease, including light chain deposition
      disease, heavy chain deposition disease, and light-and-heavy chain
      deposition disease. This branch directly incorporates proliferative
      glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits, light chain
      deposition disease, and cryoglobulinemic glomerulonephritis into a
      single unifying concept — all are driven by a clonal immunoglobulin
      product, even though the underlying hematologic disease may range from
      undetectable or monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance-level
      to overt lymphoma or Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia. This reinforces why
      light-chain and heavy-chain subclass immunofluorescence typing is
      essential whenever monoclonality is suspected.

      Branch 3 — no immunoglobulin, indicating complement- or fibrin-mediated
      disease. No significant immunoglobulin at all; the disease is driven by
      either complement dysregulation or coagulation/endothelial injury, not
      by any antibody-based process. Fibrin only indicates chronic thrombotic
      microangiopathy. C3 only indicates dense deposit disease or C3
      glomerulonephritis, distinguished from each other by electron
      microscopy (intramembranous ribbon-like versus immune complex-type
      deposits). This branch is the most conceptually distinct — no
      immunoglobulin means no antibody-driven process at all. Fibrin
      positivity should redirect the entire workup toward a thrombotic
      microangiopathy etiology rather than any form of glomerulonephritis.
      C3-only findings split into dense deposit disease versus C3
      glomerulonephritis purely by electron microscopy ultrastructure.
    bottom_line: >-
      Immunofluorescence is the master branch point for the
      membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis pattern: polyclonal
      immunoglobulin points to infection or autoimmune disease, monoclonal
      immunoglobulin points to paraprotein deposition, and absent
      immunoglobulin points to complement dysregulation or thrombotic
      microangiopathy — and a single light microscopy pattern therefore maps
      to at least three entirely different diagnostic and therapeutic
      pathways.

  - heading: "Crescentic Glomerulonephritis — The Master Three"
    question: >-
      When crescents are present on light microscopy, how do you identify the
      underlying immunopathogenic mechanism?
    content: >-
      Crescentic glomerulonephritis is a shared light microscopy endpoint
      produced by three fundamentally distinct immunopathogenic mechanisms.
      This is the final, unifying capstone framework for the proliferative
      lesions series — nearly every entity covered across lupus,
      post-infectious glomerulonephritis, IgA nephropathy and Henoch-Schonlein
      purpura, anti-glomerular basement membrane disease, and ANCA vasculitis
      can produce crescents, and immunofluorescence is the master sorting
      tool.

      | Category | Immunofluorescence pattern | Deposit/antigen basis | Representative entities |
      | --- | --- | --- | --- |
      | Immune complex-mediated | Granular (lumpy-bumpy) | Antigen-antibody complexes, polyclonal or monoclonal | Lupus nephritis III/IV, post-infectious glomerulonephritis, IgA nephropathy/Henoch-Schonlein purpura, cryoglobulinemic glomerulonephritis, fibrillary glomerulonephritis, proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits — any immune-complex disease can rarely show crescents |
      | Anti-glomerular basement membrane antibody-mediated | Linear | Antibody against intrinsic glomerular basement membrane structural antigen (NC1 domain, type IV collagen alpha-3) | Anti-glomerular basement membrane disease / Goodpasture syndrome |
      | Pauci-immune (ANCA-associated) | Negative or scant | No significant immunoglobulin or complement deposition; ANCA-driven neutrophil-mediated vessel injury | Granulomatosis with polyangiitis, microscopic polyangiitis, eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis, renal-limited ANCA vasculitis |

      Immunofluorescence alone sorts a crescentic biopsy into one of these
      three buckets almost instantly. Granular leads to immune complex
      disease, then correlate with the specific immunofluorescence
      sub-pattern — full house, IgA-dominant, C3-dominant, monoclonal — to
      identify the exact entity. Linear leads to anti-glomerular basement
      membrane disease. Negative or pauci-immune leads to ANCA vasculitis,
      then pursue MPO/PR3 serology and extrarenal clinical correlation to
      subclassify.

      The four ANCA-associated subgroups are granulomatosis with polyangiitis
      (formerly Wegener granulomatosis), microscopic polyangiitis,
      eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (formerly Churg-Strauss
      syndrome), and renal-limited ANCA vasculitis. Histopathology is
      identical across all four subgroups — subclassification depends
      entirely on extrarenal clinical features and ANCA antigen specificity
      (MPO versus PR3), not on the kidney biopsy itself.

      Two critical teaching points. First, drug-induced ANCA-associated
      vasculitis is histologically identical to primary ANCA-associated
      vasculitis; only a temporal drug history distinguishes them, and the
      pauci-immune category is not synonymous with idiopathic. Second,
      categories can overlap within a single patient: the lupus-ANCA overlap
      phenomenon demonstrates that a biopsy showing necrosis or crescents
      disproportionate to immune complex burden in a lupus patient should
      trigger ANCA serology. The three-category framework is a useful sorting
      tool, but real patients can straddle categories, and recognizing a
      mismatch between immunofluorescence findings and the severity of
      necrosis or crescents is itself a diagnostic clue pointing toward a
      possible overlap syndrome.
    bottom_line: >-
      Crescent formation is a nonspecific, severe pattern of glomerular injury
      — a final common pathway, not a diagnosis — and immunofluorescence
      alone sorts it into granular (immune complex), linear (anti-glomerular
      basement membrane), or pauci-immune (ANCA) categories, with the single
      most dangerous error being to treat "crescents" as if it were itself a
      diagnosis.

  - heading: "Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis"
    question: >-
      How do the morphologic variants of focal segmental glomerulosclerosis
      relate to primary versus secondary etiology?
    content: >-
      Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis is not one disease — it is a
      clinicopathologic syndrome unified by a shared light microscopy and
      clinical definition, but splitting into five morphologic variants, each
      with its own primary/idiopathic versus secondary etiologic spectrum.
      The single most important discriminating ultrastructural clue across
      this entire framework is the extent of foot process effacement.

      | Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis type | Foot process effacement |
      | --- | --- |
      | Idiopathic/primary | Extensive (similar in degree to minimal change disease) |
      | Secondary | Typically subtotal (less than 50%) — but exceptions occur |

      This directly echoes the very first lesson of this entire framework —
      minimal change disease's diagnostic reliance on extensive effacement —
      and confirms that the degree of foot process effacement is a genuine
      diagnostic clue to primary versus secondary focal segmental
      glomerulosclerosis, not just a nonspecific electron microscopy finding.
      However, the caveat that secondary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis
      may occasionally show extensive effacement means this rule is a guide,
      not an absolute rule; always integrate with clinical context rather
      than relying on electron microscopy alone.

      The five morphologic variants:

      | Variant | Etiology profile |
      | --- | --- |
      | Collapsing glomerulopathy | Primary/idiopathic or secondary (infections: HIV, parvovirus B19; drugs: interferon, pamidronate; severe ischemia: cocaine; autoimmune: lupus) |
      | Tip lesion | Mostly idiopathic; best prognosis, minimal change disease-like |
      | Cellular | Rarest variant |
      | Perihilar | Mostly secondary (obesity, anabolic steroids) |
      | Not otherwise specified | Idiopathic or secondary (see structural-functional adaptation below) |

      Note the inverse etiologic gradients across variants — tip lesion skews
      idiopathic, perihilar skews secondary, collapsing spans both with a
      rich secondary differential, and not otherwise specified is the default
      "everything else" category spanning the widest etiologic range.
      Recognizing which variant you are looking at should immediately narrow
      your etiologic thinking before you even reach for clinical history.

      Secondary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, not otherwise specified,
      is divided by whether the nephron number was initially reduced or
      initially normal. Category A — initially reduced nephron number:
      nephron mass is diminished from the outset, and the surviving nephrons
      hyperfilter and eventually develop secondary focal segmental
      glomerulosclerosis. Causes include unilateral renal agenesis, surgical
      ablation (nephrectomy), oligomeganephronia, chronic
      pyelonephritis/reflux nephropathy, and hypertension. Category B —
      initially normal nephron number: nephron mass starts normal, but a
      systemic metabolic or hemodynamic burden causes each nephron to work
      harder than its normal capacity, eventually producing the same
      secondary endpoint. Causes include morbid obesity, diabetes mellitus
      (though diabetic nephropathy itself is typically classified separately
      when classic Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules are present), and congestive
      heart disease.

      The unifying mechanistic concept across all secondary focal segmental
      glomerulosclerosis, not otherwise specified, causes, regardless of
      category: whether nephron number starts reduced or normal but
      overburdened, the final common pathway is the same — compensatory
      hyperfiltration in the glomeruli that remain functionally active,
      leading to maladaptive glomerular hypertrophy and eventual segmental
      sclerosis. This is the single mechanistic thread connecting reflux
      nephropathy, hypertension, and obesity.

      Alport syndrome does not appear explicitly in either category — its
      secondary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis arises through a third
      mechanism: not reduced nephron number, and not normal nephron number
      under external metabolic or hemodynamic stress, but rather structural
      glomerular basement membrane failure at the level of the individual
      nephron, causing progressive glomerular dysfunction that eventually
      manifests as focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, not otherwise
      specified. This two-category framework, while highly useful, is not
      fully exhaustive — structural and genetic collagen defects represent a
      distinct third pathway to the same endpoint.
    bottom_line: >-
      Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis is a clinicopathologic syndrome, not
      a single disease; the extent of foot process effacement guides the
      primary versus secondary distinction, the morphologic variant narrows
      the etiologic differential, and all secondary causes converge on the
      same final common pathway of compensatory hyperfiltration.

  - heading: "Nodular Mesangial Expansion — Integrated Differential Diagnosis"
    question: >-
      When nodular mesangial expansion is present on light microscopy, how do
      you work through the differential diagnosis?
    content: >-
      This represents one of the highest-yield differential diagnosis
      frameworks in renal pathology — distinct entities converging on a
      single light microscopic pattern: nodular mesangial expansion, with or
      without hypercellularity. Recognizing the pattern is easy;
      subclassifying it requires a systematic special stain, immunofluorescence,
      electron microscopy, and clinical correlation workflow, since
      hematoxylin and eosin alone cannot reliably separate them.

      | Entity | Congo red | Silver | Immunofluorescence pattern | Electron microscopy | Key clinical clue |
      | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
      | Diabetic nephropathy | Negative | Strong positive | Nonspecific | Thick glomerular basement membrane, no deposits | Diabetes history; afferent and efferent hyalinosis |
      | Light chain deposition disease | Negative | Negative or weak positive | Monoclonal kappa (glomerular basement membrane and tubular basement membrane) | Amorphous, punctate deposits | Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance / monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance / myeloma |
      | AL amyloidosis | Positive | Negative | Monoclonal kappa or lambda | Fibrils, 7–12 nm | Plasma cell dyscrasia; cardiac and nerve involvement |
      | Fibrillary glomerulonephritis | Negative | Weak or negative | Polyclonal IgG plus C3 (smudgy) | Fibrils, 12–20 nm | DNAJB9 positive; approximately 50% end-stage renal disease by 4 years |
      | Immunoglobulin deposition disease with non-amyloid organized deposits (ING) | Negative | Strong positive | Nonspecific IgG/albumin | Similar to diabetic nephropathy | No diabetes; smoking-associated |

      Practical workup when you encounter nodular mesangial sclerosis on
      hematoxylin and eosin: clinical correlation first (diabetes history,
      smoking, paraprotein); Congo red (positive indicates amyloid, then
      subtype by immunofluorescence, electron microscopy, and mass
      spectrometry); immunofluorescence for light chain restriction
      (monoclonal indicates light chain deposition disease, or amyloid if
      Congo red positive; polyclonal IgG/C3 indicates fibrillary
      glomerulonephritis); electron microscopy fibril and deposit morphology
      (organized fibrils 7–12 nm indicates amyloid, 12–20 nm indicates
      fibrillary glomerulonephritis, amorphous punctate indicates light chain
      deposition disease, no deposits with just thick glomerular basement
      membrane indicates diabetic nephropathy or immunoglobulin deposition
      disease with non-amyloid organized deposits); DNAJB9
      immunohistochemistry if fibrillary glomerulonephritis is suspected and
      electron microscopy is equivocal; and exclude diabetes clinically
      before signing out immunoglobulin deposition disease with non-amyloid
      organized deposits.
    bottom_line: >-
      Nodular mesangial expansion is a pattern, not a diagnosis; Congo red
      separates amyloid from non-amyloid, immunofluorescence separates
      monoclonal from polyclonal, and electron microscopy separates fibrils
      from amorphous deposits from no deposits at all — with clinical
      correlation required to exclude diabetes before labeling anything as
      non-diabetic.

  - heading: "Membranous Glomerulopathy Versus Amyloidosis Versus Fibrillary Glomerulonephritis"
    question: >-
      How do membranous glomerulopathy, amyloidosis, and fibrillary
