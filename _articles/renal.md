question: >-
  How should a renal glomerular biopsy be approached systematically, from
  light microscopy pattern through immunofluorescence and electron microscopy
  to a final diagnosis?

background: >-
  Light microscopy pattern is a starting point, not a diagnosis.
  Immunofluorescence is the master branch point; electron microscopy resolves
  the remainder; clinical correlation is always required. This framework
  organizes glomerular disease by the pattern seen on light microscopy and
  provides the decision logic for each pattern, from normal-appearing
  glomeruli through mesangial, endocapillary, membranoproliferative,
  crescentic, and nodular patterns.

normal_lm: >-
  PART 1 — Normal Glomeruli by Light Microscopy.

  The core concept. This category captures the most important pitfall
  category in renal biopsy interpretation: a glomerulus that looks entirely
  unremarkable on light microscopy does not mean the biopsy is normal. Both
  entities in this group are electron microscopy-dependent diagnoses. If you
  sign out "normal" based on light microscopy alone without adequate electron
  microscopy evaluation, you will miss both.

  The unifying fellow-level principle: "normal by LM" is a starting point,
  not an endpoint. Minimal change disease teaches the importance of adequate
  sampling — normal-appearing glomeruli on light microscopy could simply
  mean you have not sampled the segmentally sclerotic one (focal segmental
  glomerulosclerosis). Effacement on electron microscopy confirms podocyte
  injury is present, but you still need at least 25 glomeruli to be
  confident you are not missing focal sclerosis. Thin basement membrane
  nephropathy teaches the importance of quantitative rigor — you cannot
  eyeball glomerular basement membrane thickness; it must be measured
  against age-matched norms, using foot process width as an internal
  reference when precise morphometry is limited.

  Testable synthesis. Both entities remind you that normal light microscopy
  plus an abnormal clinical picture — nephrotic syndrome or persistent
  hematuria — is itself an indication for meticulous electron microscopy
  examination, not a reason to defer or abbreviate it.

mesangial_only: >-
  PART 2 — Mesangial Proliferation Without Nodules.

  The core concept. This category groups entities that share a light
  microscopic pattern of mesangial hypercellularity or proliferation without
  nodule formation. The critical branch point in the workup is
  immunofluorescence: does the mesangial proliferation correspond to immune
  complex deposits, or not? This single question splits the differential
  into two clean diagnostic pathways.

  Branch 1 — mesangial proliferation WITH immune complex deposits. These
  three entities can all show IgA positivity by immunofluorescence, making
  them a classic exam trap. The discriminators are: completeness of the
  immunofluorescence panel (full house = lupus); electron microscopy deposit
  location and shape (humps = infection-related; mesangial-only = IgA
  nephropathy); and clinical context (systemic lupus erythematosus history,
  recent infection, age and comorbidity profile).

  Branch 2 — mesangial proliferation WITHOUT immune complex deposits. When
  immunofluorescence is negative or nonspecific in a biopsy showing
  mesangial proliferation, your differential shifts entirely away from
  immune-complex disease and toward early sclerosing processes (diabetic
  nephropathy, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis) or a truly nonspecific
  reactive pattern — and electron microscopy becomes the deciding modality
  (thick glomerular basement membrane versus foot process effacement versus
  neither).

  Testable logic. The practical workup algorithm follows. First,
  immunofluorescence: is there a dominant immunoglobulin or complement
  pattern? If IgA-dominant, use clinical context plus electron microscopy
  deposit location (mesangial-only = IgA nephropathy; subepithelial humps =
  infection-related glomerulonephritis). If full house, lupus nephritis,
  correlate with systemic lupus erythematosus serologies. If negative or
  nonspecific, proceed to Branch 2. Second, if immunofluorescence is
  negative or nonspecific, electron microscopy for glomerular basement
  membrane thickness (diabetic nephropathy) versus foot process effacement
  with possible segmental sclerosis elsewhere in the biopsy (focal segmental
  glomerulosclerosis) versus neither (nonspecific). Third, always correlate
  clinically — diabetes history, systemic lupus erythematosus serologies,
  evidence of infection, sampling adequacy for focal segmental
  glomerulosclerosis.

mesangial_endocapillary: >-
  PART 3 — Mesangial and Endocapillary Proliferation.

  The core concept. This category groups entities that share a combined
  mesangial plus endocapillary proliferative pattern on light microscopy — a
  more active-appearing, often more acute-onset pattern than the
  mesangial-only proliferation category covered earlier. The unifying light
  microscopy theme (endocapillary hypercellularity, with or without
  crescents) means these five entities cannot be separated from each other
  by light microscopy alone. The differential hinges on immunofluorescence
  pattern (monoclonal versus polyclonal, dominant immunoglobulin) and
  electron microscopy ultrastructure (deposit location, shape, and size),
  always interpreted alongside the clinical context.

  Fellow-level pearl — the monoclonal versus polyclonal split is the
  fastest first branch point. Monoclonal immunofluorescence leads to
  proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits
  (no distinctive electron microscopy) or cryoglobulinemic
  glomerulonephritis (distinctive microtubules), narrowed further by
  electron microscopy ultrastructure and light chain and heavy chain
  restriction pattern. Polyclonal immunofluorescence leads to fibrillary
  glomerulonephritis (fibrils), post-infectious glomerulonephritis (humps),
  or lupus (full house plus multiple immunoglobulin and complement
  components), narrowed further by deposit shape and location and
  completeness of the immunoglobulin and complement panel.

  The critical "electron microscopy does the heavy lifting" principle.
  Unlike the mesangial-only category, where immunofluorescence often
  provides the decisive clue, this category leans more heavily on electron
  microscopy ultrastructure to separate look-alikes. Proliferative
  glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits is the outlier
  — it is the only entity here that is a true morphologic chameleon,
  indistinguishable from ordinary immune-complex glomerulonephritis by both
  light microscopy and electron microscopy. This is precisely why routine
  practice must include immunofluorescence light-chain and heavy-chain
  subclass staining whenever an immune-complex pattern
  glomerulonephritis is encountered, rather than assuming polyclonality by
  default.

  The practical workup algorithm follows. First, light microscopy confirms
  the pattern: mesangial plus endocapillary proliferation, with or without
  crescents, neutrophils, or cryoplugs. Second, immunofluorescence —
  monoclonal or polyclonal? If monoclonal, check electron microscopy for
  microtubules (cryoglobulinemic) versus nothing distinctive (proliferative
  glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits); correlate
  with cryoprecipitate testing and hepatitis C serology. If polyclonal,
  check immunofluorescence completeness (full house = lupus) and dominant
  component (C3-dominant or starry sky = post-infectious
  glomerulonephritis; smudgy IgG plus C3 = fibrillary
  glomerulonephritis). Third, electron microscopy ultrastructure — deposit
  location (subendothelial versus subepithelial), shape (hump versus fibril
  versus microtubule), and size (12–20 nm versus 25–35 nm) confirm or refine
  the immunofluorescence-based impression. Fourth, clinical correlation —
  systemic lupus erythematosus serologies, hepatitis C and cryoglobulin
  testing, preceding infection history, ANA and complement levels, age and
  sex demographics — essential in every case, and occasionally decisive
  when morphology is ambiguous.

mpgn_pattern: >-
  PART 4 — Membranoproliferative Glomerulonephritis Pattern.

  The core concept. This confirms and consolidates the
  membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis pattern as the final, unifying
  light microscopy category in the proliferative glomerular lesions series.
  The morphologic definition is deliberately broad and mechanism-agnostic:
  mesangial hypercellularity plus or minus endocapillary hypercellularity,
  plus GBM double contours. This single pattern is the common downstream
  output of at least five mechanistically distinct disease categories —
  which is precisely why immunofluorescence (and sometimes electron
  microscopy) is indispensable to sort them out.

  The LM definition, precisely: mesangial hypercellularity, with or without
  endocapillary hypercellularity, and splitting (double contour) of the
  glomerular basement membrane.

  Fellow-level pearl. Note that endocapillary hypercellularity is explicitly
  optional ("with or without") — a biopsy can qualify as showing a
  membranoproliferative pattern based on mesangial hypercellularity and
  double contours alone, without active endocapillary proliferation. This
  matters because the membranoproliferative pattern spans a spectrum from
  more active and proliferative to more chronic and remodeling appearances.
  Recall the Class IV lupus case with predominant double contours and only
  mild endocapillary hypercellularity.

  Why this framework is the capstone of the proliferative lesion series.
  Tracing back through this entire session, the membranoproliferative
  pattern effectively absorbs and re-contextualizes nearly every entity
  already covered. Lupus nephritis III/IV sits in the autoimmune branch
  (full house immunofluorescence, tubuloreticular aggregates).
  Cryoglobulinemic glomerulonephritis and proliferative
  glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits sit in the
  paraprotein deposition branch. Post-infectious glomerulonephritis and
  IgA-dominant infection-related glomerulonephritis sit in the
  infection-related branch, though these more classically show a purely
  endocapillary proliferative or exudative pattern rather than double
  contours — worth noting that not every infection-related
  glomerulonephritis produces full-blown membranoproliferative morphology.
  C3 glomerulonephritis and dense deposit disease sit in the complement
  regulatory protein branch. Fibrillary glomerulonephritis is technically
  distinct (organized fibrillary deposits, not a true double-contour
  membranoproliferative pattern), but shares the polyclonal IgG and C3
  immunofluorescence motif.

  Testable logic — the organizing hierarchy across this whole framework.
  Notice that thrombotic microangiopathy is explicitly cross-referenced to
  vascular lesions rather than fully elaborated here. This is a deliberate
  classification choice: thrombotic microangiopathy can produce a
  membranoproliferative-like pattern (chronic changes from endothelial
  injury) but its primary pathogenesis is vascular and endothelial, not
  glomerular and immune-complex-mediated, so it is taxonomically housed
  elsewhere despite occasionally mimicking this pattern morphologically.
  Fellows should recognize membranoproliferative-pattern injury as a
  descriptive light microscopy term, not a single diagnosis, and should
  always mentally route to the correct mechanistic category before signing
  out.

  The single decisive teaching point to carry forward: whenever double
  contours or GBM splitting are seen on light microscopy, the diagnostic
  task is never complete at the light microscopy level. Immunofluorescence
  (immunoglobulin clonality, dominant component) plus or minus electron
  microscopy (deposit location and character) plus or minus clinical and
  serologic correlation (infection screen, autoimmune serologies,
  paraprotein workup, complement studies) are all required to assign the
  correct final diagnosis and, critically, the correct treatment pathway
  (antimicrobial or antiviral therapy, immunosuppression, anti-complement
  therapy, or hematologic treatment, respectively).

mpgn_if_classification: >-
  PART 5 — Immunofluorescence-Based Classification of the Membranoproliferative Pattern.

  The core concept. This is the capstone organizing principle for everything
  covered across the lupus, cryoglobulinemic, post-infectious, fibrillary,
  monoclonal immunoglobulin deposition, C3 glomerulonephritis, and dense
  deposit disease discussions. The membranoproliferative pattern by light
  microscopy (mesangial plus endocapillary hypercellularity, GBM double
  contours) is a shared morphologic endpoint reached by mechanistically
  distinct diseases. Immunofluorescence is the master branch point that
  sorts this single light microscopy pattern into three fundamentally
  different disease categories, and this is the single most efficient
  framework for approaching any membranoproliferative-pattern biopsy in
  practice.

  Branch 1 — polyclonal immunoglobulin plus C3/C1q, indicating immune
  complex disease. Genuine antibody-antigen immune complexes, polyclonal in
  nature, driven by an external trigger (infection) or systemic autoimmune
  process. Infection-related examples include hepatitis B, hepatitis C,
  endocarditis, shunt infections, visceral abscesses, leprosy, malaria,
  schistosomiasis, and mycoplasma. Autoimmune-related examples include
  systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjogren syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, and
  mixed connective tissue disease. Fellow-level pearl: this branch is a
  reminder that membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis is very often
  secondary, not idiopathic — a biopsy with polyclonal full-house-type
  immunofluorescence should trigger a broad infectious and autoimmune
  serologic workup, not an assumption of primary or idiopathic
  membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, a diagnosis that has become far
  less common as more secondary causes are identified.

  Branch 2 — monoclonal immunoglobulin plus C3/C1q, indicating paraprotein
  deposition disease. Immune complexes or deposits containing a clonal
  immunoglobulin component, reflecting an underlying clonal B-cell or plasma
  cell process spanning the full monoclonal gammopathy of renal
  significance-to-malignancy spectrum. The clonal process may be monoclonal
  gammopathy of undetermined significance, Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia,
  chronic lymphocytic leukemia, or low-grade B-cell lymphoma. Specific
  disease patterns include cryoglobulinemia types 1 and 2,
  immunotactoid/fibrillary glomerulopathy, and monoclonal immunoglobulin
  deposition disease, including light chain deposition disease, heavy chain
  deposition disease, and light-and-heavy chain deposition disease. Testable
  logic: this branch directly incorporates proliferative
  glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits, light chain
  deposition disease, and cryoglobulinemic glomerulonephritis into a single
  unifying concept — all are driven by a clonal immunoglobulin product,
  even though the underlying hematologic disease may range from undetectable
  or monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance-level to overt
  lymphoma or Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia. This reinforces why
  light-chain and heavy-chain subclass immunofluorescence typing is
  essential whenever monoclonality is suspected.

  Branch 3 — no immunoglobulin, indicating complement- or fibrin-mediated
  disease. No significant immunoglobulin at all — the disease is driven by
  either complement dysregulation or coagulation and endothelial injury, not
  by any antibody-based process. Fibrin only indicates chronic thrombotic
  microangiopathy. C3 only indicates dense deposit disease or C3
  glomerulonephritis, distinguished from each other by electron microscopy
  (intramembranous ribbon-like versus immune complex-type deposits).
  Fellow-level pearl: this branch is the most conceptually distinct — no
  immunoglobulin means no antibody-driven process at all. Fibrin positivity
  should redirect the entire workup toward a thrombotic microangiopathy
  etiology rather than any form of glomerulonephritis. C3-only findings
  split into dense deposit disease versus C3 glomerulonephritis purely by
  electron microscopy ultrastructure.

  Testable logic — the master exam concept. A single light microscopy
  pattern (membranoproliferative) maps to at least three entirely different
  diagnostic and therapeutic pathways, and immunofluorescence is the
  indispensable tool that separates them. Light microscopy alone is never
  sufficient, and even within each branch, further discrimination requires
  clinical correlation (serologies, hematologic workup) or electron
  microscopy (for the C3-only branch specifically).

mpgn_if_classification_bottom_line: >-
  Immunofluorescence is the master branch point for the membranoproliferative
  pattern: polyclonal points to infection or autoimmune disease, monoclonal
  points to paraprotein deposition, and absent immunoglobulin points to
  complement dysregulation or thrombotic microangiopathy.

crescentic: >-
  PART 6 — Crescentic Glomerulonephritis: The Master Three.

  The core concept. Crescentic glomerulonephritis is a shared light
  microscopy endpoint (crescent formation, often with fibrinoid necrosis)
  produced by three fundamentally distinct immunopathogenic mechanisms.
  This is the final, unifying capstone framework for the entire proliferative
  lesions series — nearly every entity covered across lupus,
  post-infectious glomerulonephritis, IgA nephropathy and Henoch-Schonlein
  purpura, anti-glomerular basement membrane disease, and ANCA vasculitis
  can produce crescents, and immunofluorescence is the master sorting tool,
  exactly as it has been for the membranoproliferative pattern and the
  nodular and membranous patterns before it.

  Fellow-level pearl — the fastest possible triage of any crescentic GN
  biopsy. Immunofluorescence alone sorts a crescentic biopsy into one of
  these three buckets almost instantly. Granular leads to immune complex
  disease, then correlate with the specific immunofluorescence sub-pattern —
  full house, IgA-dominant, C3-dominant, monoclonal — to identify the exact
  entity. Linear leads to anti-glomerular basement membrane disease.
  Negative or pauci-immune leads to ANCA vasculitis, then pursue MPO/PR3
  serology and extrarenal clinical correlation to subclassify.

  The four ANCA-associated subgroups are granulomatosis with polyangiitis
  (formerly Wegener granulomatosis), microscopic polyangiitis, eosinophilic
  granulomatosis with polyangiitis (formerly Churg-Strauss syndrome), and
  renal-limited ANCA vasculitis. Recall: histopathology is identical across
  all four subgroups. Subclassification depends entirely on extrarenal
  clinical features and ANCA antigen specificity (MPO versus PR3), not on
  the kidney biopsy itself.

  Two critical "don't get fooled" teaching points from this series. First,
  drug-induced ANCA-associated vasculitis is histologically identical to
  primary ANCA-associated vasculitis. Only a temporal drug history
  distinguishes them — the pauci-immune category is not synonymous with
  idiopathic. Second, categories can overlap within a single patient. The
  lupus-ANCA overlap phenomenon demonstrates that a biopsy showing necrosis
  or crescents disproportionate to immune complex burden in a lupus patient
  should trigger ANCA serology — the three-category framework is a useful
  sorting tool, but real patients can straddle categories, and recognizing a
  mismatch between immunofluorescence findings and the severity of necrosis
  or crescents is itself a diagnostic clue pointing toward a possible
  overlap syndrome.

  Testable logic — the ultimate synthesis of this entire framework.
  Crescent formation is a nonspecific, severe pattern of glomerular injury —
  a final common pathway, not a diagnosis. Just as nodular mesangial
  expansion, the membranoproliferative pattern, and the membranous pattern
  each required immunofluorescence (plus or minus electron microscopy, plus
  or minus clinical correlation) to resolve into a specific diagnosis,
  crescentic glomerulonephritis requires the same disciplined,
  immunofluorescence-first approach. The single most dangerous
  pattern-matching error in renal pathology is treating crescents as if they
  were themselves a diagnosis, rather than recognizing them as the shared
  endpoint of at least three (and often overlapping) distinct disease
  mechanisms.

fsgs: >-
  PART 7 — Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis.

  The core concept. This capstone framework brings together every
  focal segmental glomerulosclerosis-related entity from this framework
  into a single organizing structure. Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis is
  not one disease — it is a clinicopathologic syndrome unified by a shared
  light microscopy and clinical definition, but splitting into five
  morphologic variants, each with its own primary or idiopathic versus
  secondary etiologic spectrum. The single most important discriminating
  ultrastructural clue across this entire framework is the extent of foot
  process effacement.

  The defining feature: foot process effacement as a primary versus
  secondary clue. Idiopathic or primary focal segmental
  glomerulosclerosis shows extensive effacement, similar in degree to
  minimal change disease. Secondary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis
  typically shows subtotal effacement (less than 50%), but exceptions
  occur.

  Fellow-level pearl. This directly echoes the very first lesson of this
  entire framework — minimal change disease's diagnostic reliance on
  extensive effacement — and confirms that the degree of foot process
  effacement is a genuine diagnostic clue to primary versus secondary focal
  segmental glomerulosclerosis, not just a nonspecific electron microscopy
  finding. However, the caveat that secondary focal segmental
  glomerulosclerosis may occasionally show extensive effacement means this
  rule is a guide, not an absolute rule — always integrate with clinical
  context (obesity, reflux history, hypertension, etc.) rather than relying
  on electron microscopy alone.

  Testable logic. Note the inverse etiologic gradients across variants —
  tip lesion skews idiopathic, perihilar skews secondary, collapsing spans
  both with a rich secondary differential, and not otherwise specified is
  the default everything-else category spanning the widest etiologic range.
  Recognizing which variant you are looking at should immediately narrow
  your etiologic thinking before you even reach for clinical history.

  The FSGS-NOS secondary etiology framework — a structural logic. This is
  the most valuable organizing principle in this section: secondary focal
  segmental glomerulosclerosis, not otherwise specified, is divided by
  whether the nephron number was initially reduced or initially normal.

  Category A — initially reduced nephron number. Nephron mass is diminished
  from the outset; the surviving nephrons hyperfilter and eventually develop
  secondary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis. Causes include unilateral
  renal agenesis, surgical ablation (nephrectomy), oligomeganephronia,
  chronic pyelonephritis and reflux nephropathy, and hypertension.

  Category B — initially normal nephron number. Nephron mass starts normal,
  but a systemic metabolic or hemodynamic burden causes each nephron to work
  harder than its normal capacity, eventually producing the same secondary
  endpoint. Causes include morbid obesity, diabetes mellitus (though
  diabetic nephropathy itself is typically classified separately from
  focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, not otherwise specified, when classic
  Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules are present), and congestive heart disease.

  Fellow-level pearl — the unifying mechanistic concept across all
  secondary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, not otherwise specified,
  causes, regardless of category. Whether nephron number starts reduced
  (Category A) or normal but overburdened (Category B), the final common
  pathway is the same: compensatory hyperfiltration in the glomeruli that
  remain functionally active, leading to maladaptive glomerular hypertrophy
  and eventual segmental sclerosis. This is the single mechanistic thread
  connecting reflux nephropathy, Alport syndrome (a variant on reduced
  effective nephron function from structural failure), hypertension, and
  obesity — all different starting points converging on the same
  hyperfiltration-mediated secondary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis.

  Placing Alport syndrome within this framework. Interestingly, Alport
  syndrome does not appear explicitly in either Category A or B — its
  secondary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis arises through a third
  mechanism: not reduced nephron number, and not normal nephron number under
  external metabolic or hemodynamic stress, but rather structural
  glomerular basement membrane failure at the level of the individual
  nephron, causing progressive glomerular dysfunction that eventually
  manifests as focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, not otherwise specified.
  This is worth flagging as a reminder that this two-category framework,
  while highly useful, is not fully exhaustive — structural and genetic
  collagen defects represent a distinct third pathway to the same endpoint.

nodular: >-
  PART 8 — Nodular Mesangial Expansion: Integrated Differential Diagnosis.

  The core concept. This list represents one of the highest-yield
  differential diagnosis frameworks in renal pathology — distinct entities
  converging on a single light microscopic pattern: nodular mesangial
  expansion, with or without hypercellularity. Recognizing the pattern is
  easy; subclassifying it requires a systematic special stain,
  immunofluorescence, electron microscopy, and clinical correlation
  workflow, since hematoxylin and eosin alone cannot reliably separate them.

  The practical workup algorithm. When you encounter nodular mesangial
  sclerosis on hematoxylin and eosin, work through in this order. First,
  clinical correlation: diabetes history? smoking? paraprotein? Second,
  Congo red: positive equals amyloid, then subtype by immunofluorescence,
  electron microscopy, and mass spectrometry — AL, ALECT2, AA, and so on.
  Third, immunofluorescence for light chain restriction: monoclonal equals
  light chain deposition disease, or amyloid if Congo red positive;
  polyclonal IgG and C3 equals fibrillary glomerulonephritis. Fourth,
  electron microscopy fibril and deposit morphology: organized fibrils at
  7–12 nm equal amyloid, 12–20 nm equal fibrillary glomerulonephritis,
  amorphous punctate equals light chain deposition disease, no deposits with
  just thick glomerular basement membrane equals diabetic nephropathy or
  immunoglobulin deposition disease with non-amyloid organized deposits.
  Fifth, DNAJB9 immunohistochemistry if fibrillary glomerulonephritis is
  suspected and electron microscopy is equivocal. Sixth, exclude diabetes
  clinically before signing out immunoglobulin deposition disease with
  non-amyloid organized deposits.

mgp_amyloid_fgn: >-
  PART 9 — Membranous Glomerulopathy Versus Amyloidosis Versus Fibrillary
  Glomerulonephritis.

  Membranous glomerulopathy, amyloidosis, and fibrillary
  glomerulonephritis can all present with nephrotic-range proteinuria, and
  each can recur after transplantation. Hematuria favours fibrillary
  glomerulonephritis and is uncommon in membranous glomerulopathy, which is
  the key clinical discriminator. Amyloidosis is distinguished by Congo red
  positivity and by fibril diameter on electron microscopy. The three
  entities differ substantially in demographic profile, clinical course,
  and post-transplant behaviour.
