article_type: "Pathology framework"

question: >-
  How does myocardial ischemia progress from coronary plaque to myocardial
  infarction and scar, and how do morphologic features guide the dating of
  infarction, recognition of complications, and clinical management?

background: >-
  Ischemic heart disease is an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and
  demand. It is the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for
  approximately nine million deaths per year, with more than half of the fall
  in mortality in high-income countries attributable to prevention, therapy,
  and lifestyle change. The principal cause is obstructive atherosclerosis of
  the epicardial coronary arteries. Stable angina and acute coronary syndromes
  are not points on a single spectrum of stenosis severity: they are distinct
  clinical entities with different plaque biology, different presentations,
  and different management. This framework follows the disease from etiology
  and plaque biology through the ischemic myocardium, the morphologic
  evolution of infarction, reperfusion and its consequences, initial
  management, complications, and synthesis. It is written for postgraduate
  pathology review.

foundations_question: >-
  What is myocardial ischemia, and what is the spectrum of clinical syndromes
  it produces?

foundations: >-
  Myocardial ischemia is inadequate perfusion relative to metabolic demand.
  The principal cause is obstructive atherosclerosis of the major epicardial
  coronary arteries, so ischemic heart disease is often used synonymously with
  coronary artery disease. Ischemia differs from pure hypoxemia because
  substrate delivery and metabolite washout are also impaired, and ischemic
  injury therefore evolves faster than hypoxic injury alone. Lesions favour
  proximal segments and branch points of the left anterior descending, left
  circumflex, and right coronary arteries.

  Other causes include vasospasm, including drug-induced spasm from cocaine
  or ephedrine; coronary embolism from atrial fibrillation, left ventricular
  thrombus, endocarditis, or prosthetic valves; vasculitis and dissection,
  including Kawasaki disease and spontaneous coronary artery dissection;
  microvascular disease in diabetes and hypertension; and low perfusion
  pressure from shock, hypotension, or hypoxemia.

  Precipitants include tachycardia, which raises demand and shortens diastole
  when most left ventricular perfusion occurs; myocardial hypertrophy, which
  raises demand and reduces capillary density; anemia and hypoxemia, which
  reduce oxygen content; and systemic hypotension, which reduces coronary
  perfusion pressure. Tachycardia is therefore doubly harmful.

  The clinical spectrum ranges from angina pectoris, including stable,
  Prinzmetal, and unstable forms, through myocardial infarction and chronic
  ischemic heart disease with heart failure, to sudden cardiac death from a
  lethal arrhythmia within approximately one hour of symptoms.

foundations_bottom_line: >-
  Ischemic heart disease is a supply-demand imbalance; the principal cause is
  epicardial coronary atherosclerosis, but precipitants and non-atherosclerotic
  causes must be considered in every case.

pathogenesis_question: >-
  What distinguishes a stable atherosclerotic plaque from a vulnerable plaque,
  and how does acute plaque change produce an acute coronary syndrome?

pathogenesis: >-
  There are two separate routes to myocardial ischemia, and they should not
  be conflated.

  The first route is chronic atherosclerotic narrowing, which produces fixed
  stenosis. Stenosis of at least 70% causes ischemia on exertion, and critical
  stenosis of at least 90% causes ischemia even at rest. Because the process
  is slow, collaterals can develop and limit ischemia. A stable plaque of this
  kind causes predictable angina, may remain quiescent for years, and does not
  itself progress to an acute coronary syndrome simply by enlarging. Its
  fibrous cap is thick, its lipid core is small, and inflammation is low.

  The second route is acute plaque change, which produces the acute coronary
  syndromes. A vulnerable plaque has a thin fibrous cap, a large necrotic
  lipid core, and heavy inflammation. Rupture, fissure, ulceration, or erosion
  of this plaque exposes thrombogenic content, leading to platelet activation
  and thrombosis. Complete occlusion typically produces a transmural infarct
  with ST elevation; partial or transient occlusion produces unstable angina
  or a subendocardial infarct without ST elevation. Crucially, the culprit
  lesion in acute coronary syndrome is often only moderately stenotic before
  the event, and stenosis severity poorly predicts which plaque will rupture.
  The stable lesion causing angina and the vulnerable lesion that ruptures are
  frequently different plaques in the same patient.

  Statins stabilize plaques through reduced inflammation and improved
  endothelial function, a benefit that extends beyond LDL cholesterol
  lowering.

  Plaque rupture and plaque erosion are distinct mechanisms of acute plaque
  change. Rupture accounts for the majority of coronary thrombi, is
  characterized by a thin fibrous cap less than 65 micrometres that is
  disrupted, a large necrotic lipid core with many macrophages, and a
  fibrin-rich and red cell-rich thrombus, and is typical in older men with
  classic risk factors. Erosion accounts for approximately one-quarter to
  one-third of thrombi, has an intact fibrous cap with denuded endothelium,
  little or no lipid core with a proteoglycan-rich and smooth muscle
  cell-rich plaque, a platelet-rich thrombus with neutrophils and neutrophil
  extracellular traps, and is more typical in younger patients, women, and
  smokers presenting with non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndrome. At
  autopsy, cutting the culprit segment at three to five millimetre intervals
  and taking step sections through the thrombus base helps separate rupture
  from erosion.

  Vulnerability extends beyond the plaque. Three domains contribute: a
  vulnerable plaque, characterized by inflammation, extent, severity, and
  location of disease; thrombogenic blood, characterized by inflammation,
  comorbidities such as diabetes and chronic kidney disease, environmental
  factors such as smoking, and genetic background; and vulnerable myocardium,
  determined by the duration and extent of ischemia, individual
  susceptibility, and microvascular injury. Inflammation is the common thread
  across all three domains and provides the rationale for anti-inflammatory
  strategies in coronary artery disease. This model explains why two patients
  with identical angiograms can have very different outcomes.

  The Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction distinguishes
  myocardial injury from myocardial infarction. Myocardial injury is a cardiac
  troponin above the 99th percentile upper reference limit, acute if there is
  a rise or fall. Myocardial infarction is acute injury plus clinical evidence
  of ischemia, including symptoms, new ischemic electrocardiographic changes
  or Q waves, imaging evidence of loss of viable myocardium or new regional
  wall-motion abnormality, or thrombus at angiography or autopsy. The five
  types are Type 1, atherothrombotic plaque disruption with thrombus, the
  classic spontaneous acute coronary syndrome; Type 2, supply-demand mismatch
  without acute atherothrombosis; Type 3, cardiac death with suggestive
  symptoms before biomarkers are available; Type 4, PCI-related, with 4a for
  PCI-related, 4b for stent thrombosis, and 4c for restenosis; and Type 5,
  CABG-related.

  Myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries, or MINOCA,
  is a working diagnosis. Causes include fixed plaques unmasked by a
  supply-demand imbalance; non-atherosclerotic coronary disease such as
  spasm, small-vessel disease, vasculitis, dissection, embolism, congenital
  anomalies, fibromuscular dysplasia, iatrogenic causes, and PCI-related
  no-reflow; non-coronary cardiac imbalance such as sustained tachyarrhythmia
  or bradyarrhythmia and left ventricular hypertrophy or dilatation;
  extracardiac imbalance such as respiratory failure, severe anemia,
  hypovolemic or septic shock; and other causes including sickle cell disease,
  amyloidosis with intramural vessel involvement, and catecholamine excess
  from cocaine or pheochromocytoma. Myocarditis and takotsubo syndrome must be
  excluded, usually by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, before assigning a
  cause. Sampling intramural vessels widely and considering elastic stains for
  dissection and vasculitis and Congo red for amyloid are important
  histopathologic steps.

  The thrombotic cascade proceeds in five steps. Plaque disruption exposes
  collagen and the necrotic core. Platelet adhesion and aggregation release
  thromboxane A2, ADP, and serotonin, which promote further aggregation and
  vasospasm. Tissue factor activates the coagulation cascade, generating
  thrombin and fibrin. Thrombus occludes the lumen within minutes.
  Irreversible injury follows after 20 to 30 minutes of severe ischemia.
  Thrombus is found in approximately 90% of transmural infarcts examined
  early, and early reperfusion can abort progression. Each step is a drug
  target: aspirin for thromboxane A2, P2Y12 inhibitors for ADP, glycoprotein
  IIb/IIIa inhibitors for the final common pathway, anticoagulants for
  thrombin, and fibrinolysis or percutaneous coronary intervention for
  occlusion.

pathogenesis_bottom_line: >-
  Stable and vulnerable plaques are different lesions with different biology;
  acute coronary syndromes arise from acute plaque change with thrombosis,
  often at a site that was not critically stenotic before the event.

ischemic_myocardium_question: >-
  Once coronary flow is interrupted, how quickly does the myocardium become
  irreversibly injured, and in what pattern does necrosis spread?

ischemic_myocardium: >-
  The myocardial response to ischemia follows a predictable timeline. Within
  seconds, anaerobic glycolysis replaces oxidative metabolism, ATP falls, and
  lactate rises. Within one minute, contractility is lost, but this is
  reversible. Over minutes, myofibrils relax, glycogen is lost, and cells and
  mitochondria swell. At 20 to 30 minutes, irreversible injury begins.
  Without reperfusion, necrosis of the full area at risk is complete by 6 to
  12 hours.

  The earliest necrosis occurs in the subendocardium, which is last to be
  perfused, is subject to the highest wall stress, and has the fewest
  collaterals. Necrosis then advances as a wavefront from subendocardium
  toward epicardium over hours. Reperfusion in the first few hours salvages
  the still-viable outer wall, which is the basis of door-to-balloon targets.

  Three patterns of infarction are recognized. Transmural infarction follows
  complete and persistent occlusion of an epicardial artery, involves the full
  wall thickness within one coronary territory, and is usually caused by
  plaque rupture with occlusive thrombus. Subendocardial or non-transmural
  infarction follows partial or transient occlusion or global hypoperfusion,
  involves the inner one-third to one-half of the wall, and is circumferential
  if global; common causes include lysis of an occlusive thrombus and
  prolonged shock or hypotension. Multifocal micro-infarction follows disease
  of small intramural vessels, is microscopic and scattered, and is caused by
  microemboli, vasculitis, spasm, or catecholamine toxicity. A circumferential
  subendocardial infarct that is not confined to one territory points to
  global hypoperfusion rather than a single culprit artery. Catecholamine
  excess, as with cocaine, can cause sudden death or, with repeated insults,
  chronic ischemic cardiomyopathy.

  The major coronary territories are summarized in the table. In a
  right-dominant heart, the left anterior descending artery supplies 40 to 50%
  of infarcts and perfuses the anterior left ventricular wall near the apex,
  the anterior two-thirds of the septum, and the apex circumferentially, with
  corresponding anterior, anteroseptal, and apical changes on leads V1 to V4.
  The right coronary artery supplies 30 to 40% and perfuses the right
  ventricular free wall, the inferior and posterior left ventricle, and the
  posterior one-third of the septum, corresponding to inferior and posterior
  changes with or without right ventricular involvement on leads II, III, aVF,
  and V4R. The left circumflex artery supplies 15 to 20% and perfuses the
  lateral left ventricular wall except the apex, corresponding to lateral
  changes on leads I, aVL, V5, and V6, or inferoposterior changes if
  left-dominant. Other territories account for less than 5% and include the
  left main, diagonal, and obtuse marginal branches. Isolated right
  ventricular infarction is rare and accounts for 1 to 3%. Atrial infarction
  is rare and usually accompanies right or circumflex occlusion. Inferior
  ST-elevation myocardial infarction with hypotension and clear lungs
  suggests right ventricular infarction, in which case right-sided leads
  should be recorded, nitrates avoided, and fluids given cautiously.

ischemic_myocardium_bottom_line: >-
  Necrosis begins subendocardially at 20 to 30 minutes and advances as a
  wavefront to the epicardium over 6 to 12 hours; timing determines salvage,
  and the pattern of infarction reflects the underlying mechanism.

morphologic_evolution_question: >-
  Can an infarct be dated reliably from its gross and microscopic appearance,
  and what are the pitfalls in dating it at autopsy?

morphologic_evolution: >-
  Infarct morphology evolves in a predictable sequence that allows dating.
  In the first half hour, there is no gross or light microscopic change,
  although electron microscopy shows reversible injury. Between half an hour
  and four hours, wavy fibres may appear at the border, but the infarct is
  otherwise invisible; tetrazolium stains reveal infarcts at least two to
  three hours old. Between four and 24 hours, dark mottling appears grossly,
  and microscopy shows early coagulative necrosis with hypereosinophilic
  cytoplasm and pyknotic nuclei, interstitial edema, and focal hemorrhage,
  with neutrophils appearing by 12 to 24 hours. Between one and three days,
  mottling with a yellow-tan centre is seen grossly, with loss of nuclei and
  cross-striations and a brisk neutrophilic infiltrate. Between three and ten
  days, the infarct becomes maximally yellow-tan and soft, with disintegration
  of dead myofibres, dying neutrophils, macrophages, and early granulation
  tissue. Between ten days and eight weeks, granulation tissue with new
  vessels and collagen develops, followed by a red-gray depressed border and
  progression to a gray-white scar. After two months, a dense firm gray-white
  scar is present, the wall may be thinned, and dense collagen with few
  fibroblasts and vessels replaces myocardium.

  Healing is centripetal, so the centre of a large infarct can look younger
  than its edge, and the infarct should be dated from its most advanced area.
  Cardiomyocytes are terminally differentiated and do not regenerate, so once
  healed a scar cannot be dated precisely.

  Wavy fibres are non-specific and are also seen with agonal stretching and
  artefact; myocardial infarction should never be diagnosed on waviness alone.
  Hypereosinophilia alone is weak evidence and should be judged against
  adjacent myocardium on the same slide. A new friction rub on day two to
  three after a transmural infarct suggests early post-infarction pericarditis
  rather than Dressler syndrome.

  The histologic dating features are summarized in the table. Stretched and
  wavy fibres appear at one to two hours and disappear thereafter.
  Coagulative necrosis with hypereosinophilia begins at one to three hours,
  reaches full development at one to three days with loss of striations, and
  disintegrates after three days. Interstitial edema appears at four to 12
  hours. Nuclear changes begin at 12 to 24 hours with pyknosis and
  karyorrhexis, are fully developed at one to three days with loss of nuclei,
  and thereafter depend on infarct size. Neutrophil infiltration begins at 12
  to 24 hours, is fully developed at one to three days, and decreases by five
  to seven days. Neutrophil karyorrhexis begins at one and a half to two days
  and peaks at three to five days. Macrophages and lymphocytes appear at three
  to five days, are fully developed at five to ten days including
  siderophages, and persist from ten days to two months. Vessel and
  endothelial sprouts appear at five to ten days, are fully developed at ten
  days to four weeks, and decrease after four weeks, though some large dilated
  vessels may persist. Fibroblasts and young collagen appear at five to ten
  days, are fully developed at two to four weeks, and decrease after four
  weeks depending on infarct size, with dense fibrosis eventually replacing
  them.

  Tetrazolium stains allow gross detection of early infarcts. Tetrazolium
  salts, including triphenyltetrazolium chloride (red) and nitroblue
  tetrazolium (blue-purple), are reduced by dehydrogenases in viable
  myocardium to a coloured formazan, whereas infarcted muscle has lost these
  enzymes and cofactors and remains pale. The window is infarcts at least two
  to three hours old, before changes are visible on routine gross or
  hematoxylin and eosin examination. The technique involves fresh unfixed
  transverse slices of about one centimetre incubated in buffered stain at 37
  degrees Celsius and photographed promptly. Limitations include false
  positives with a long post-mortem interval from autolysis, abolition of
  staining by fixation, and patchy staining of reperfused infarcts. The stain
  should be applied before formalin fixation, and the pale zone and its
  border should be sampled for histology.

  Autopsy practice for suspected myocardial infarction follows a systematic
  approach. Coronary arteries are cut transversely at three to five millimetre
  intervals along the main epicardial arteries, and heavily calcified segments
  are decalcified. Stenosis is recorded per segment together with any
  thrombus, rupture, erosion, or dissection. The myocardium is bread-loafed in
  transverse slices of about one centimetre from apex to about two centimetres
  below the atrioventricular valves, sampling each coronary territory, any
  lesion and its border, and the papillary muscles. Pitfalls include
  contraction band necrosis, which can also result from resuscitation or
  defibrillation, catecholamines, and handling artefact; wavy fibres and
  isolated hypereosinophilia, which are non-specific; autolysis, which
  produces pale fragmented myocytes that should not be over-interpreted;
  death within about four to six hours, in which morphology may be absent and
  reliance must be placed on tetrazolium stains, the coronary lesion, and
  history; and ancillary immunohistochemistry such as C5b-9, C4d, and
  fibronectin, which can highlight early injury but is not fully validated.

morphologic_evolution_bottom_line: >-
  Infarct morphology evolves predictably from invisible injury through
  coagulative necrosis, neutrophilic infiltration, macrophage phagocytosis,
  granulation tissue, and scar; healing is centripetal and the infarct should
  be dated from its most advanced area.

reperfusion_question: >-
  How does restoring flow alter the morphology of an infarct, and what is the
  difference between stunned and hibernating myocardium?

reperfusion: >-
  The objective of reperfusion is to salvage ischemic but not-yet-necrotic
  myocardium, chiefly the outer portion of the area at risk. The principle is
  that time is myocardium: the earlier reperfusion is achieved, the greater
  the salvage. Methods include fibrinolysis with fibrin-specific agents such
  as tenecteplase or alteplase; percutaneous coronary intervention with
  balloon angioplasty and stenting, which is preferred when timely; and
  coronary artery bypass grafting for selected anatomy or mechanical
  complications. Salvage falls steeply after the first hours, but reperfusion
  is still indicated up to 12 hours from onset in ST-elevation myocardial
  infarction, and longer when ischemia is ongoing.

  Reperfusion injury is mediated by several mechanisms. Mitochondrial
  dysfunction follows opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition
  pore, with outer membrane rupture releasing cytochrome c and other
  pro-apoptotic proteins, leading to apoptosis and necrosis. Myocyte
  hypercontracture follows restoration of ATP in cells with high cytosolic
  calcium, producing unregulated actin-myosin contraction and contraction
  band necrosis. Reactive oxygen species generated by oxygen influx cause
  lipid and protein oxidation and membrane damage. Leukocytes and the
  no-reflow phenomenon, through neutrophil plugging, endothelial swelling,
  microemboli, proteases, and elastases, produce microvascular obstruction.
  Platelets and complement amplify endothelial injury and extend ischemic
  damage.

  The morphology of the reperfused infarct differs from the non-reperfused
  infarct. Grossly, the infarct is often hemorrhagic from vascular leakage and
  microvascular rupture. The hallmark is contraction band necrosis, with
  dense eosinophilic transverse bands of hypercontracted sarcomeres reflecting
  calcium influx on reperfusion. Inflammation arrives earlier and healing is
  faster than in non-reperfused infarcts, so standard dating tables apply less
  well. Distal embolization of plaque or thrombus debris during percutaneous
  coronary intervention can cause micro-infarcts. Hemorrhage and contraction
  bands in a non-territorial pattern should raise consideration of
  resuscitation or catecholamine effect before being attributed to
  reperfusion.

  Stunned and hibernating myocardium are distinct entities. Stunned
  myocardium follows brief severe ischemia with reperfusion, so perfusion is
  restored but function is transiently depressed, recovering spontaneously
  over hours to days. Left ventricular dysfunction early after primary
  percutaneous coronary intervention may improve, and left ventricular
  ejection fraction should be reassessed before decisions about implantable
  cardioverter-defibrillators. Hibernating myocardium follows chronic
  sublethal hypoperfusion, with persistently reduced perfusion and adaptively
  down-regulated metabolism and contractility, recovering after
  revascularization. It is identified by viability imaging, including
  dobutamine echocardiography, positron emission tomography, and cardiac
  magnetic resonance imaging, in ischemic cardiomyopathy.

reperfusion_bottom_line: >-
  Reperfusion salvages the outer area at risk but produces hemorrhage,
  contraction band necrosis, microvascular obstruction, and faster atypical
  healing; stunned and hibernating myocardium are distinct reversible states.

management_question: >-
  What is the physiologic rationale for initial therapy in acute myocardial
  infarction, and how is the reperfusion strategy chosen?

management: >-
  Initial therapy follows physiologic logic. Oxygen is given only if oxygen
  saturation is below 90% or there is respiratory distress; routine oxygen
  gives no benefit. Nitrates relieve pain and spasm and reduce preload but
  should be avoided in hypotension, right ventricular infarction, and recent
  phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor use. Antiplatelet therapy consists of an
  aspirin load followed by low-dose aspirin plus a P2Y12 inhibitor, with
  ticagrelor or prasugrel preferred when percutaneous coronary intervention is
  planned, and dual antiplatelet therapy for approximately 12 months by
  default. Parenteral anticoagulation is given to all patients, with
  unfractionated heparin in primary percutaneous coronary intervention and
  enoxaparin, fondaparinux, or bivalirudin by context. An oral beta-blocker is
  given within 24 hours if there is no heart failure, low-output state, shock
  risk, bradycardia, or atrioventricular block. Continuous electrocardiographic
  monitoring is used, with treatment of ventricular fibrillation,
  ventricular tachycardia, and atrioventricular block, and correction of
  anemia, hypoxemia, pain, and anxiety.

  Reperfusion strategy in ST-elevation myocardial infarction follows time
  targets. Primary percutaneous coronary intervention is preferred if it can
  be achieved within 120 minutes of first medical contact, with a target
  first-medical-contact-to-device time of 90 minutes or less, or 120 minutes
  or less if inter-hospital transfer is required. Radial access and
  culprit-vessel stenting are standard, and complete revascularization of
  non-culprit lesions is performed in most stable patients. Fibrinolysis is
  used when percutaneous coronary intervention cannot be delivered within 120
  minutes and symptoms are within 12 hours, given within 30 minutes of
  arrival, or within 10 minutes of diagnosis in the European Society of
  Cardiology pathway, with a fibrin-specific agent. Transfer for angiography
  follows at two to 24 hours after successful lysis, with rescue percutaneous
  coronary intervention if lysis fails, defined as less than 50% ST resolution
  at 60 to 90 minutes. In non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndrome, timing of
  angiography is risk-based: immediate within two hours for very high risk
  including shock, refractory pain, life-threatening arrhythmia, or mechanical
  complication, and early within 24 hours for high risk.

  Secondary prevention includes high-intensity statin therapy started early,
  angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers
  particularly when left ventricular ejection fraction is 40% or less or in
  heart failure, diabetes, or hypertension, and mineralocorticoid receptor
  antagonists when left ventricular ejection fraction is 40% or less with
  heart failure or diabetes.

management_bottom_line: >-
  Initial management restores supply-demand balance, limits infarct size, and
  prevents complications; reperfusion strategy in ST-elevation myocardial
  infarction is time-critical and follows defined first-medical-contact
  targets.

complications_question: >-
  How does the timing of each complication of myocardial infarction relate to
  the underlying morphology?

complications: >-
  Complications follow a timeline anchored to infarct morphology. In the
  first minutes to one hour, sudden death from ventricular fibrillation is the
  predominant risk, reflecting electrical instability of ischemic muscle.
  From hours to days, arrhythmia, pump failure, shock, and right ventricular
  infarction occur, reflecting necrosis and loss of contractile mass. On days
  one to three, fibrinous pericarditis develops over a transmural infarct.
  From days three to seven, free wall, septal, and papillary muscle rupture
  occur, reflecting maximal softening before collagen deposition. Over days to
  weeks, mural thrombus, remodeling, and Dressler syndrome develop, reflecting
  stasis, endocardial injury, and autoimmunity. Over weeks to years, true
  aneurysm, chronic heart failure, and scar-related ventricular tachycardia
  develop, reflecting thin collagenous scar and remodeling.

  Immediate and early complications include sudden death and arrhythmia.
  Many ST-elevation myocardial infarction deaths occur before hospitalization,
  mostly from ventricular fibrillation within the first hour. Arrhythmias
  occur in up to approximately 90% of myocardial infarctions and include sinus
  bradycardia, atrioventricular block, supraventricular tachycardia, premature
  ventricular complexes, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation,
  and asystole, with highest risk in the first hour and declining thereafter.
  Contractile dysfunction produces left ventricular failure proportional to
  infarct size, with hypotension, pulmonary congestion, and edema.
  Cardiogenic shock occurs in approximately 10% of transmural infarctions,
  typically with at least 40% of the left ventricle infarcted, and is managed
  by urgent revascularization and hemodynamic support as needed. Right
  ventricular infarction usually accompanies proximal right coronary artery
  occlusion in inferior or posterior infarction and presents with hypotension,
  raised jugular venous pressure, and clear lungs, with Kussmaul sign
  sometimes present. It is preload-dependent, so nitrates and diuretics should
  be avoided, fluids given, and atrioventricular block monitored.

  Mechanical complications from myocardial rupture include free wall rupture,
  which causes hemopericardium and tamponade, often presenting with sudden
  pulseless electrical activity or shock; septal rupture, which causes acute
  ventricular septal defect and left-to-right shunt, presenting with a new
  harsh holosystolic murmur and thrill; and papillary muscle rupture, which
  causes acute severe mitral regurgitation, often with flash pulmonary edema
  and a murmur that may be soft. These occur classically on days three to
  seven at the time of maximal softening, though they may occur earlier after
  late reperfusion. Incidence was historically one to five percent and is now
  less than one percent in the primary percutaneous coronary intervention era,
  but mortality remains very high. Risk factors include age over 60, female
  sex, first infarction without collaterals, anterior or lateral transmural
  infarct, absence of left ventricular hypertrophy, and late or no
  reperfusion.

  Pericarditis, thrombus, and aneurysm form a further group. Early
  pericarditis occurs on days one to three, is fibrinous or
  fibrinothermorrhagic over a transmural infarct, and presents with pleuritic
  pain and a friction rub that is usually self-limited. Mural thrombus occurs
  over days to weeks from stasis, endocardial injury, and dilation, and may
  cause systemic embolism such as stroke; anticoagulation is indicated when
  present. True aneurysm occurs over weeks to months, is a wide-necked
  outpouching lined by thin scar, and may cause heart failure, ventricular
  tachycardia, or mural thrombus, though rupture is rare. Pseudoaneurysm
  occurs over days to weeks as a contained free wall rupture with a wall of
  pericardium or thrombus and a narrow neck, carries a high risk of rupture,
  and requires urgent surgery. Dressler syndrome occurs at approximately two
  to eight weeks and is an autoimmune pericarditis from antibodies to
  myocardial or pericardial antigens, presenting with fever, pleuritic pain,
  and effusion, now rare in the reperfusion era.

  Late complications include remodeling and heart failure. Infarct expansion
  is stretching and thinning of the necrotic segment, leading to chamber
  dilation. Non-infarcted myocardium undergoes compensatory hypertrophy,
  interstitial fibrosis, and increased sphericity. These changes are initially
  adaptive and ultimately maladaptive, raising wall stress and oxygen demand.
  Progressive chronic heart failure reflects cumulative loss of contractile
  mass, remodeling, and ischemia of viable muscle, producing ischemic
  cardiomyopathy. Therapy that limits remodeling includes
  angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers,
  angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors, beta-blockers,
  mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2
  inhibitors when heart failure with reduced ejection fraction is present.

  Prognosis after myocardial infarction is approximately one to two percent
  in-hospital mortality overall, first-year mortality mostly within 30 days,
  and annual mortality thereafter. Poor prognostic factors include a large,
  anterior, or transmural infarct; low left ventricular ejection fraction;
  heart failure or shock; advanced age over 70; female sex; diabetes; prior
  myocardial infarction; and chronic kidney disease. Prevention includes
  primary prevention through control of risk factors before a first event and
  secondary prevention through smoking cessation, cardiac rehabilitation,
  high-intensity statin therapy with ezetimibe or PCSK9 inhibitor to
  low-density lipoprotein cholesterol target, antiplatelet therapy,
  beta-blockade, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibition or angiotensin
  receptor blockade, and blood pressure and glycemic control.

complications_bottom_line: >-
  Complications of myocardial infarction follow a timeline anchored to infarct
  morphology: arrhythmia in the first hour, pump failure in the first days,
  rupture at days three to seven during maximal softening, and aneurysm,
  thrombus, and scar-related arrhythmia in the weeks to years thereafter.

synthesis_question: >-
  Can the pathogenesis, morphology, and clinical course of myocardial
  infarction be integrated into a single framework, and how does that
  framework apply to individual patients?

synthesis: >-
  Ischemic heart disease is an imbalance of oxygen supply and demand, and
  therapy either restores supply or reduces demand. Stable and vulnerable
  plaques are different lesions: stable plaques cause predictable angina and
  do not themselves progress to acute coronary syndrome, whereas vulnerable
  plaques undergo acute change, rupture or erosion with thrombosis, often at
  a site that was not critically stenotic. Necrosis begins subendocardially at
  20 to 30 minutes and advances epicardially over 6 to 12 hours, so timing
  determines salvage. Morphology dates the infarct: neutrophils at one to
  three days, macrophages at three to ten days, granulation tissue at one to
  two weeks, and scar by two months. Morphology also predicts complications,
  with softening at days three to seven explaining rupture and thin scar
  explaining aneurysm, thrombus, and ventricular tachycardia. Reperfusion
  salvages muscle but produces hemorrhage, contraction bands, and faster,
  atypical healing.

  Case-based review. A 64-year-old man with a late-presenting anterior
  ST-elevation myocardial infarction without reperfusion suddenly develops
  hypotension, raised jugular venous pressure, and pulseless electrical
  activity on day five. The likely cause is free wall rupture with tamponade,
  and histology at days three to seven would show disintegrating myofibres and
  macrophage phagocytosis with little collagen, corresponding to maximal
  softening.

  A 70-year-old man with inferior ST-elevation myocardial infarction becomes
  hypotensive after sublingual nitroglycerin, with clear lungs and raised
  jugular venous pressure. The likely diagnosis is right ventricular
  infarction from proximal right coronary artery occlusion. Management is to
  stop nitrates, give intravenous fluids because the right ventricle is
  preload-dependent, pursue urgent reperfusion, and monitor for
  atrioventricular block.

  A 55-year-old woman dies four days after primary percutaneous coronary
  intervention, with a grossly hemorrhagic infarct and dense transverse
  eosinophilic bands in myocytes. The change is contraction band necrosis from
  reperfusion, caused by calcium influx into injured myocytes with restored
  ATP producing hypercontracture, and hemorrhage reflects microvascular
  injury.

  A 78-year-old woman in septic shock with heart rate 140 per minute has a
  rising and falling high-sensitivity troponin, with angiography showing
  stable non-obstructive plaques. This is Type 2 myocardial infarction from
  supply-demand mismatch, not atherothrombotic Type 1. Management is to treat
  the precipitant, and antithrombotic therapy for acute coronary syndrome is
  not routinely indicated.

synthesis_bottom_line: >-
  Ischemic heart disease runs from supply-demand imbalance through acute
  plaque change and wavefront necrosis to morphologic evolution, reperfusion
  injury, and complications whose timing is anchored to infarct morphology.
