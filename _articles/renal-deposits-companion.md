question: >-
  What do the terms used in renal biopsy reports actually mean, and why does
  the way a deposit forms determine where it sits and what it looks like
  under the electron microscope?

background: >-
  The companion framework article organizes glomerular disease by light
  microscopy pattern and uses immunofluorescence and electron microscopy to
  reach a diagnosis [1]. That approach relies on a specialized vocabulary —
  subendothelial, monotypic, C3-dominant, organized deposits, effacement —
  that is rarely defined in one place. This article supplies those
  definitions and explains the underlying logic: deposits reach the glomerulus
  by a small number of routes, and each route leaves a characteristic
  signature in location, immunofluorescence composition, and ultrastructure.
  Once the route is understood, the structure seen on electron microscopy
  stops being a list of facts to memorize and becomes a consequence of
  mechanism. A section on commonly confused terms addresses pairs of names
  that differ by a single word but describe unrelated diseases, including
  dense deposit disease and light chain deposition disease.

filter_anatomy_question: >-
  What are the layers of the glomerular filter, and why does it matter which
  layer a deposit sits in?

filter_anatomy: >-
  The glomerulus is a tuft of capillaries that filters blood into urine. From
  the blood side to the urine side, each capillary wall has three layers. The
  endothelium lines the capillary and is perforated by open windows called
  fenestrae. The glomerular basement membrane (GBM) is a sheet of
  extracellular matrix, mainly type IV collagen, that forms the structural
  barrier; on electron microscopy it has a dark central layer (lamina densa)
  flanked by paler inner and outer layers (lamina rara interna and
  externa). The podocytes sit on the urine side and wrap the capillaries with
  interdigitating foot processes, bridged by the slit diaphragm, which is the
  final barrier to protein.

  The capillaries are held together by a central stalk called the mesangium,
  made of mesangial cells and their matrix. The mesangium is not separated
  from the blood by a basement membrane, so material in the circulation
  reaches it easily. The whole tuft sits inside Bowman's capsule, lined by
  parietal epithelial cells; when these proliferate in response to severe
  injury, they form a crescent.

  Location matters because of access. Anything on the blood side of the GBM —
  in the mesangium or beneath the endothelium (subendothelial) — is exposed
  to circulating inflammatory cells and complement, and tends to cause
  inflammation and proliferation. Anything on the urine side
  (subepithelial) is shielded from the circulation by the GBM, so it causes
  little inflammation but damages podocytes and produces protein leak. This
  single idea explains much of the difference between nephritic and
  nephrotic presentations.

  shielded from blood and injure podocytes, causing nephrotic proteinuria.

reading_the_biopsy_question: >-
  What does each of the three main biopsy techniques show, and what do the
  common descriptive terms in a report mean?

reading_the_biopsy: >-
  A renal biopsy is examined with three complementary techniques, and each
  answers a different question.

  Light microscopy answers the question of pattern: which compartments are
  abnormal and how. Several stains are used together. Hematoxylin and eosin
  gives the overall picture. Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) stains basement
  membranes and mesangial matrix. Jones methenamine silver stains the GBM
  black and shows spikes and double contours. Trichrome stains immune
  deposits red and fibrosis blue. Congo red identifies amyloid, which shows
  apple-green birefringence when viewed under polarized light.

  Immunofluorescence answers the question of composition: what the deposits
  are made of. Frozen tissue is stained with fluorescent antibodies against
  IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C1q, kappa, lambda, and fibrinogen, with IgG subclasses
  and specific antigens as needed. The distribution of staining is described
  as granular (discrete lumps, typical of immune complexes), linear (a smooth
  continuous line, typical of antibodies bound to the GBM itself or of
  monoclonal immunoglobulin deposition disease), or smudgy (ill-defined,
  typical of fibrillary glomerulonephritis). Intensity is graded from
  negative through trace to 3+. When frozen tissue is unavailable, or when
  monoclonal deposits are suspected but not seen, immunofluorescence can be
  performed on paraffin sections after digestion with the enzyme pronase,
  which unmasks immunoglobulin that frozen-section staining can miss [9].

  Electron microscopy answers the question of structure: exactly where the
  deposits sit and what they are made of at the nanometer scale. Deposits
  appear dark because they scatter electrons, and are described as
  electron-dense. Most immune complex deposits are amorphous or granular.
  Some deposits have an organized substructure — fibrils, microtubules, or
  crystals — and the presence, size, and arrangement of that substructure
  is one of the strongest clues to the underlying disease.

immune_complex_question: >-
  How do immune complexes reach the glomerulus, and why do their routes lead
  to different locations and different clinical syndromes?

immune_complex: >-
  An immune complex is an antibody bound to its antigen. Immune complexes are
  the most common cause of glomerular deposits, and they reach the
  glomerulus in two ways [2].

  In the first route, complexes form in the circulation and are trapped as
  blood passes through the glomerulus. Large complexes cannot cross the GBM,
  so they lodge on the blood side, in the mesangium or the subendothelial
  space. There they activate complement and attract inflammatory cells,
  causing proliferation, inflammation, and a nephritic picture with
  hematuria. Lupus nephritis and the immune complex forms of the
  membranoproliferative pattern work largely this way, as does IgA
  nephropathy, in which abnormally glycosylated IgA forms complexes that
  settle in the mesangium.

  In the second route, complexes form in place. Antibodies cross the GBM and
  bind to antigens on the podocyte surface, or to antigens that have become
  planted there. The resulting subepithelial deposits are separated from
  the blood by the GBM, so inflammatory cells cannot reach them. Complement
  is still activated, and its terminal membrane attack complex injures the
  podocyte directly, causing heavy proteinuria with little inflammation.
  This is the mechanism of membranous nephropathy, in which the target
  antigen is most often phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R) [3].

  Anti-GBM disease is a special case. The antibody targets a structural
  component of the GBM itself, a domain of type IV collagen, which is
  distributed evenly along the membrane. The antibody therefore coats the
  GBM in a smooth line on immunofluorescence and produces no discrete deposits
  on electron microscopy. Pauci-immune disease, by contrast, shows little
  or no immunoglobulin at all; injury in ANCA vasculitis is caused by
  activated neutrophils rather than by deposited antibody.

  Complexes built from a monoclonal antibody behave in the same way as
  ordinary immune complexes. Proliferative glomerulonephritis with
  monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits (PGNMID) produces granular deposits
  that look like any other immune complex disease on light and electron
  microscopy, and is recognized only because immunofluorescence shows a
  single light chain and a single IgG subclass [7].

monoclonal_question: >-
  How can a small amount of a single abnormal antibody damage the kidney, and
  why does the same basic protein produce fibrils in one patient, powder in
  another, and crystals in a third?

monoclonal: >-
  Antibodies are built from heavy chains and light chains. Each plasma cell
  makes one antibody, and each antibody uses only one type of light chain,
  kappa or lambda. A healthy immune response involves many plasma cell
  families and produces a polyclonal mixture, so tissue deposits contain
  both kappa and lambda. When one plasma cell or B-cell family expands into a
  clone, it produces a single identical protein, a monoclonal
  immunoglobulin, which may be a whole antibody or a free light or heavy
  chain.

  Most people with a small monoclonal protein have monoclonal gammopathy of
  undetermined significance (MGUS), which usually causes no harm. Monoclonal
  gammopathy of renal significance (MGRS) describes a clone that produces a
  nephrotoxic monoclonal immunoglobulin but does not meet the hematological
  criteria for treating a specific malignancy [4,5]. The clone is small, but
  the kidney damage is real and often progressive, which is why these
  patients may need treatment directed at the clone even without a
  malignancy [6].

  Whether a monoclonal protein damages the kidney, and how, depends mainly on
  its amino acid sequence rather than its quantity. Small changes in the
  variable region of a light chain alter how stably it folds, how it
  interacts with other molecules, and whether it resists being broken down.
  Those physical properties decide the lesion. An unstable light chain that
  partly unfolds can stack into rigid fibrils with a cross-beta sheet
  structure, which is amyloid. A light chain that deposits as fine
  non-organized granules along basement membranes produces light chain
  deposition disease. A heavy chain missing the domain that normally binds
  its light chain can be secreted alone and deposit in the same way, as heavy
  chain deposition disease. Some whole immunoglobulins precipitate in the
  cold and assemble into microtubules, which are cryoglobulins. Some light
  chains resist digestion inside proximal tubule cells and form crystals.

  Pathologists group these lesions by the structure of the deposit. Organized
  deposits have a regular internal architecture visible on electron
  microscopy: fibrils, microtubules, or crystals. Non-organized deposits are
  granular or powdery. The distinction narrows the differential quickly, but
  it does not by itself prove monoclonality: fibrillary glomerulonephritis
  has organized fibrils yet is usually polyclonal, and AA and ALECT2 amyloid
  are organized but contain no immunoglobulin at all.

  Two further points complete the picture. First, a monoclonal protein can
  damage the kidney without depositing much of itself, by disrupting
  complement regulation (C3 glomerulopathy associated with a monoclonal
  gammopathy) or by injuring the endothelium (thrombotic microangiopathy)
  [5]. Second, cast nephropathy, the classic kidney injury of myeloma, is
  largely a matter of quantity: large amounts of free light chain bind to
  uromodulin in the distal tubule and form obstructing casts. Because it
  requires a high tumor burden, cast nephropathy is a myeloma-defining event
  and falls outside the definition of MGRS.

complement_question: >-
  How does complement normally stay in check, and how does its failure
  produce deposits that contain C3 but almost no antibody?

complement: >-
  Complement is a cascade of blood proteins that tags and destroys
  pathogens. It can be triggered in three ways: by antibodies (the classical
  pathway, which uses C1q), by sugar patterns on microbes (the lectin
  pathway), and by continuous low-level spontaneous activation (the
  alternative pathway). All three converge on C3, the central component.
  Once cleaved, C3 fragments coat nearby surfaces and amplify further
  activation.

  Because the alternative pathway is always ticking over, it must be
  continuously restrained. Factor H, a circulating regulator, and factor I,
  which cleaves C3 fragments, perform this role. Cells also carry their own
  membrane-bound regulators. The GBM is acellular, lacks those membrane
  regulators, and depends on circulating factor H for protection, which
  makes it unusually vulnerable when regulation fails.

  Regulation can fail in several ways: inherited variants in factor H or
  related proteins, autoantibodies such as C3 nephritic factor that
  stabilize the enzyme that cleaves C3, or a monoclonal immunoglobulin that
  interferes with a regulator. The result is uncontrolled cleavage of C3 in
  the circulation and the deposition of C3 fragments in the glomerulus
  without meaningful antibody. This is C3 glomerulopathy [8].

  On immunofluorescence, C3 glomerulopathy is defined by C3-dominant
  staining, meaning C3 at least two grades brighter than any
  immunoglobulin on the 0 to 3+ scale [8]. Electron microscopy then splits it
  into two diseases. In dense deposit disease, the GBM itself is transformed
  by very dark, ribbon-like intramembranous deposits. In C3
  glomerulonephritis, the deposits are less dense and discrete, lying in the
  mesangium and capillary walls.

  Two situations mimic C3 glomerulopathy. Post-infectious
  glomerulonephritis can be C3-dominant because the antibody component is
  cleared early; it is usually transient and follows infection. Masked
  monoclonal deposits can also appear C3-dominant on frozen-section
  immunofluorescence, and pronase digestion of paraffin sections reveals the
  hidden immunoglobulin [9].

confused_terms_question: >-
  Which pairs of terms sound alike but describe different things, and how
  can they be told apart?

confused_terms: >-
  Much confusion in renal pathology arises from names that share words but
  describe unrelated processes. The most common trap involves the word
  dense. Every deposit seen on electron microscopy is electron-dense, so the
  phrase electron-dense deposit is a description, not a diagnosis. Dense
  deposit disease is a specific complement disease in which the GBM is
  transformed by very dark intramembranous material that contains C3 but no
  immunoglobulin. Light chain deposition disease is an unrelated monoclonal
  disease in which a single light chain deposits as fine powdery material
  along the inner GBM and the tubular basement membranes. There is no
  disease called light chain dense deposit disease; the phrase usually
  reflects a merging of these two names.

  The two diseases can be separated with a single stain. Dense deposit
  disease shows C3 with little or no immunoglobulin. Light chain deposition
  disease shows linear staining for one light chain, usually kappa, along
  both glomerular and tubular basement membranes, with little complement.

  - pair: "Electron-dense deposit versus dense deposit disease"
    distinction: "The first is any dark deposit on electron microscopy; the second is a specific C3 glomerulopathy with very dark intramembranous ribbons"
  - pair: "Dense deposit disease versus light chain deposition disease"
    distinction: "C3 without immunoglobulin, deposits within the GBM, versus a single light chain, powdery deposits along the inner GBM and tubular basement membranes"
  - pair: "Deposition disease versus deposits"
    distinction: "Monoclonal immunoglobulin deposition disease names a specific linear, powdery monoclonal disease; deposits is a general word for any abnormal material"
  - pair: "PGNMID versus MIDD"

  - pair: "Monotypic versus monoclonal"

  - pair: "Immunotactoid glomerulopathy versus cryoglobulinemic glomerulonephritis"

  - pair: "Membranous versus membranoproliferative"

  - pair: "C3 glomerulopathy versus C3 glomerulonephritis"

  - pair: "Hyaline thrombus versus hyalinosis"
    distinction: "Immune material plugging a capillary lumen, versus glassy plasma protein in arteriolar walls in diabetes or hypertension"
  - pair: "Subendothelial versus subepithelial"
    distinction: "Blood side of the GBM, causing inflammation, versus urine side of the GBM, causing protein leak"

confused_terms_bottom_line: >-
  Electron-dense describes any dark deposit; dense deposit disease is a C3
  disease within the GBM; light chain deposition disease is a monoclonal
  disease along basement membranes. No disease is called light chain dense
  deposit disease.

    location: "Mesangium and capillary walls"
    if: "Smudgy polyclonal IgG and C3; DNAJB9 positive"
    em: "Randomly arranged fibrils, averaging about 20 nm"
    examples: "Fibrillary glomerulonephritis"
  - mechanism: "Complement dysregulation"
    location: "Within the GBM, mesangial, capillary wall"
    if: "C3-dominant; little or no immunoglobulin"
    em: "Very dark intramembranous ribbons, or less dense discrete deposits"
    examples: "Dense deposit disease, C3 glomerulonephritis"
  - mechanism: "Neutrophil-mediated injury"
    location: "No deposits"
    if: "Negative or scant"
    em: "No deposits; necrosis"
    examples: "ANCA-associated vasculitis"

mechanism_structure_bottom_line: >-
  Location reveals how a deposit arrived, immunofluorescence reveals what it
  is made of, and ultrastructure reveals whether it assembled by itself;
  together they point back to the mechanism.

detection_ladder_question: >-
  In what order should the tests be performed to move from a suspected
  deposit disease to a named protein and its source?

detection_ladder:
  - "Light microscopy with special stains defines the pattern: PAS, silver, and trichrome for structure, and Congo red whenever amyloid is possible."
  - "Immunofluorescence on frozen tissue defines composition: IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C1q, kappa, lambda, fibrinogen, then IgG subclasses and antigens such as PLA2R as needed."
  - "Immunofluorescence on pronase-digested paraffin sections is added when frozen tissue is inadequate, when a C3-dominant pattern is found, or when a monoclonal lesion is suspected but staining is negative."
  - "Electron microscopy defines location and ultrastructure: amorphous, granular, powdery, fibrillar, microtubular, or crystalline, with measurement of any fibrils or tubules."
  - "Ancillary tissue tests resolve remaining questions: mass spectrometry to type amyloid or identify unusual proteins, and DNAJB9 immunohistochemistry for suspected fibrillary glomerulonephritis."
  - "Blood and urine tests identify the circulating protein: serum and urine protein electrophoresis with immunofixation and serum free light chains when a monoclonal lesion is present; complement levels, C3 nephritic factor, and complement genetics for C3 glomerulopathy."
  - "Bone marrow examination, with flow cytometry where needed, identifies the clone in MGRS, because treatment is directed at the clone."

detection_ladder_bottom_line: >-
  Each step answers a narrower question: pattern, composition, hidden
  deposits, structure, protein identity, circulating protein, and finally the
  clone or complement defect responsible.

glossary_question: >-
  What do the specialized terms used in this article and the companion
  framework mean?

glossary_intro: >-
  The following glossary defines terms used in this article and in the
  companion framework. Definitions are deliberately brief; the sections above
  give the context.

glossary:
  - term: "AA amyloidosis"
    meaning: "Amyloid formed from serum amyloid A, an inflammatory protein; seen in chronic inflammatory disease"
  - term: "AL amyloidosis"
    meaning: "Amyloid formed from a monoclonal light chain"
  - term: "ALECT2 amyloidosis"
    meaning: "Amyloid formed from leukocyte chemotactic factor 2; not immunoglobulin-related"
  - term: "Alternative pathway"
    meaning: "The complement pathway that activates continuously at a low level and must be held in check by regulators"
  - term: "ANCA"
    meaning: "Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, directed against MPO or PR3; cause pauci-immune vasculitis"
  - term: "APOL1"
    meaning: "A gene whose high-risk variants, common in people of West African ancestry, increase the risk of focal segmental glomerulosclerosis"
  - term: "Birefringence"
    meaning: "The property of changing color under polarized light; apple-green birefringence on Congo red identifies amyloid"
  - term: "C1q"
    meaning: "The first component of the classical complement pathway; its presence suggests antibody-triggered complement activation"
  - term: "C3"
    meaning: "The central complement protein where all three pathways converge"
  - term: "C3-dominant"
    meaning: "C3 staining at least two grades brighter than any immunoglobulin; the defining immunofluorescence finding of C3 glomerulopathy"
  - term: "C3 nephritic factor"
    meaning: "An autoantibody that stabilizes the enzyme cleaving C3, causing uncontrolled complement activation"
  - term: "Cast nephropathy"
    meaning: "Tubular obstruction by casts of free light chain bound to uromodulin; a myeloma-defining event"
  - term: "Clone"
    meaning: "A family of cells descended from one ancestor and making one identical protein"
  - term: "Congo red"
    meaning: "A stain that binds amyloid"
  - term: "Crescent"
    meaning: "Proliferating cells filling Bowman's space after capillary rupture"
  - term: "Cryoglobulin"
    meaning: "An immunoglobulin that precipitates below body temperature"
  - term: "DNAJB9"
    meaning: "A protein found in the deposits of fibrillary glomerulonephritis; its immunohistochemical staining confirms the diagnosis"
  - term: "Effacement"
    meaning: "Flattening of podocyte foot processes; the structural sign of podocyte injury"
  - term: "Factor H"
    meaning: "The main circulating regulator of the alternative complement pathway"
  - term: "Fenestrae"
    meaning: "Open windows in the glomerular endothelium"
  - term: "Fibrils"
    meaning: "Long, thin, solid nonbranching structures within deposits, measured in nanometers"
  - term: "GBM"
    meaning: "Glomerular basement membrane; the collagen-rich structural layer of the capillary wall"
  - term: "Heavy chain"
    meaning: "The larger of the two chains that make up an antibody; defines the class (IgG, IgA, IgM) and subclass"
  - term: "IgG subclass"
    meaning: "One of four types of IgG (IgG1 to IgG4); subclass restriction is a clue to monoclonality or to the type of membranous nephropathy"
  - term: "Immune complex"
    meaning: "An antibody bound to its antigen"
  - term: "In situ formation"
    meaning: "Formation of immune complexes in the tissue itself, rather than in the circulation"
  - term: "Kappa and lambda"
    meaning: "The two types of light chain; each antibody uses only one"
  - term: "Lamina densa"
    meaning: "The dark central layer of the GBM on electron microscopy"
  - term: "Light chain"
    meaning: "The smaller of the two chains that make up an antibody"
  - term: "Masked deposits"
    meaning: "Monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits invisible on routine frozen-section immunofluorescence and revealed by pronase digestion"
  - term: "Membrane attack complex"
    meaning: "The terminal complement complex (C5b-9) that punches holes in cell membranes; injures podocytes in membranous nephropathy"
  - term: "Mesangium"
    meaning: "The central stalk of the glomerular tuft"
  - term: "MGRS"
    meaning: "Monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance: a clone making a nephrotoxic protein without meeting criteria for malignancy treatment"
  - term: "MGUS"
    meaning: "Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance: a small clone without organ damage"
  - term: "Microtubules"
    meaning: "Hollow tube-shaped structures within deposits; seen in cryoglobulinemic and immunotactoid disease"
  - term: "Monotypic"
    meaning: "Tissue staining restricted to one light chain type or one heavy chain subclass"
  - term: "Nephritic syndrome"
    meaning: "Hematuria, hypertension, and reduced kidney function, reflecting glomerular inflammation"
  - term: "Nephrotic syndrome"
    meaning: "Heavy proteinuria, low serum albumin, and edema, reflecting podocyte or filter injury"
  - term: "Organized deposits"
    meaning: "Deposits with a regular internal structure (fibrils, microtubules, crystals) visible on electron microscopy"
  - term: "Pauci-immune"
    meaning: "Showing little or no immunoglobulin or complement on immunofluorescence"
  - term: "PLA2R"
    meaning: "Phospholipase A2 receptor, a podocyte protein and the most common target antigen in membranous nephropathy"
  - term: "Podocyte"
    meaning: "The epithelial cell on the urine side of the capillary wall"
  - term: "Polyclonal"
    meaning: "Produced by many cell families; tissue deposits contain both kappa and lambda"
  - term: "Pronase"
    meaning: "An enzyme used to digest paraffin sections so that hidden immunoglobulin can be stained"
  - term: "Slit diaphragm"
    meaning: "The membrane bridging adjacent podocyte foot processes; the final barrier to protein"
  - term: "Subendothelial"
    meaning: "Between the endothelium and the GBM, on the blood side"
  - term: "Subepithelial"
    meaning: "Between the GBM and the podocyte, on the urine side"
  - term: "Uromodulin"
    meaning: "A protein secreted by distal tubule cells, also called Tamm-Horsfall protein; binds light chains to form casts"

glossary_bottom_line: >-
  Most terms in a renal biopsy report describe one of three things: where a
  deposit sits, what it is made of, or how it is built.

bottom_line: >-
  Deposits reach the glomerulus as immune complexes, as monoclonal proteins
  acting through their own chemistry, or through runaway complement; where
  they sit, what they contain, and how they are built reveal which route
  produced them.
